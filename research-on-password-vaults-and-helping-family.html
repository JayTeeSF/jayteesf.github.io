Password vaults, and what happens when someone needs help

19 September 2026

We read the security papers, the vendor whitepapers and the source code for every password vault we could find, open and closed. The finding that surprised us: no vault can hand your passwords to a family member the way people assume. The companies say so in writing. The vaults that do let someone else in mostly manage it with a sheet of paper, or with a timer that only protects you while you are well enough to object. Meanwhile the thing that actually goes wrong is far more ordinary — somebody forgets, and everything is gone.

The short answer

People imagine a password vault as a safe deposit box: if something happens, your daughter takes the key to the bank and they let her in. That is not how any of them work.

Every mechanism we found moves one of three things, and none of them is "your passwords":

  • Your data — photos, mail, documents. This is what Apple, Google and Facebook will release, and they are explicit that passwords are not included.
  • Control of a vault — a password manager hands the whole vault to a person you named in advance, by replacing the master password. This works. It is also, mechanically, an account takeover.
  • A secret you already split up — the mathematical schemes. They work, and they ask more of ordinary people than ordinary people can reliably do.

The useful reframing came from a 2021 research paper, and it is the one idea we would want anyone to take away:

"for some digital items, the problem of navigating access after death can be avoided by arranging and implementing access for specific individuals during life. In these cases, the challenge may be reframed as one of organisation and communication, rather than one of planning."

Holt, Nicholson & Smeddinck, The Web Conference 2021

In other words: the version that works is the boring one. Share access now, with someone you trust, while everyone is well. Everything clever we looked at is an attempt to avoid having that conversation, and everything clever we looked at is worse.

What the big companies will and will not hand over

All four of the platforms most families already use have some kind of feature for this. None of them gives anyone your passwords.

What each platform releases to a person you nominate
PlatformFeaturePasswords?
AppleLegacy ContactNo — excluded by design
GoogleInactive Account ManagerNo — "We cannot provide passwords or other login details"
FacebookLegacy ContactNo
MicrosoftNoneNo feature at all; a court order, then deletion

Apple's is the most interesting, because the exclusion is not a policy someone could be persuaded to bend. Their own documentation lists what a Legacy Contact cannot reach:

"Movies, music, books, or subscriptions you purchased with your Apple Account, and data stored in your iCloud Keychain (payment information, passwords, and passkeys)"

Apple Support, support.apple.com/en-us/102631

And their Platform Security guide explains that it is built into the keys themselves — "the keying information used by a beneficiary doesn't encompass the information necessary to decrypt the decedent's iCloud Keychain." Apple could not override this if it wanted to. A court order cannot produce something Apple cannot decrypt.

Microsoft has nothing. Its process routes families to a subpoena, warns that even then it may not be able to help, and deletes inactive accounts after two years. For a family, the realistic outcome is that the account is simply gone.

The practical consequence. Set up Apple Legacy Contact, Google Inactive Account Manager and Facebook Legacy Contact for everyone in your family today. They are free, they take minutes, and they solve the photos-and-email problem. Just do not mistake them for a plan about passwords, because they are not one.

Which vaults can let a trusted person in

This is where the vaults genuinely differ, and where the marketing is least helpful. We sorted every product by what its feature actually is, reading the cryptography where it was published and the source code where it was not.

1Password's is worth quoting, because "Emergency Kit" sounds like software and is not:

"Your Emergency Kit is a piece of paper (once you've printed it) … It also has space for you to write your account password."

1Password security whitepaper

That is not a criticism of paper. Paper survives decades, needs no company to still exist, and asks the holder to understand nothing — which, as we will see, counts for a great deal. It is a criticism of the word "kit".

The catch that applies to all of the good ones

In every vault in the top half of that table, the key is protected by mathematics but the release is protected by the company. The vendor holds the sealed package and decides when the timer has run out. They cannot read it. They do decide when to hand it over.

And the timer has a flaw that is structural rather than fixable. Here is Bitwarden's own description:

"If the account holder can't log in, your request is automatically approved once the wait time expires."

Bitwarden documentation

Read that carefully. The protection is that you will notice the request and refuse it. The feature fires precisely when you cannot notice or refuse. The safeguard is anti-correlated with the event it is supposed to guard. Worse, rejecting a request does not remove the person or stop them asking again — so an attacker can retry forever while you have to win every single time.

Self-hosting has its own version of this. Vaultwarden gives Bitwarden's feature away free, but the timer is a scheduled job in a configuration file. If the machine is switched off or the scheduler disabled at the moment the timer runs out, the status never changes and the person you nominated is locked out permanently. The families least able to guarantee a server stays running are exactly the families who most need this to just work.

Forgetting is the real risk

Almost everything written about password managers is about attackers. In a family, the overwhelmingly likely failure is that someone forgets their master password on an ordinary Tuesday.

Here is what each product does at that moment:

  • Apple destroys the escrow record after ten failed attempts. Their words: "the keychain is lost forever."
  • Google's on-device encryption cannot be removed once enabled, and the reset deletes every saved password.
  • NordPass's last-resort reset "will delete all of your NordPass items."
  • Bitwarden, Enpass and Dashlane cannot reset a vault at all.
  • Padloc's recovery flow deletes the vault — and it is still selling a family plan with mobile apps last updated in 2022.

Across everything we examined, only two designs let a trusted relative fix a forgotten password without destroying the data: 1Password's family plan, where a second organiser cryptographically holds the vault keys, and Keeper's 24-word recovery phrase.

That is the gap. Not "what happens in thirty years" — what happens on a Tuesday.

And the evidence says the backup plans people are told to make do not exist. In a 2025 study, 37 people were given a 1Password licence for three months:

"Only 5 out of the 37 participants wrote their primary password in the emergency kit … Interestingly, 4 people did not know where they stored the emergency kit."

Arias Cabarcos & Mayer, USENIX SOUPS 2025

A separate study of an encrypted-mail provider found only 14.8% of users stored their recovery code in more than one place, and 12.7% believed customer support could rescue them. It cannot.

What the research says about people over 60

We expected this section to be about decline. It is not, and we were wrong about it.

On several measures older adults do better. A study of 579 people found older users "more likely to generate secure passwords and show proactive risk awareness and regularly install updates." Another found younger people were more likely to share passwords, not less. And a 2021 study of 26 people over 60 found that once they adopt a password manager they are "tenacious users … engage in more secure practices rather than simply convenient ones."

Three real findings survive, and all three are about design rather than ability.

1. The fear is failure, not hackers

"older adults in our study were afraid of putting their trust in the technology which may fail, rather than their account being 'hacked.'"

Ray, Wolf, Kuber & Aviv, USENIX Security 2021

Given the list of recovery cliffs above, this fear is correct. It should be answered with a design that cannot lose your data, not with reassurance.

2. Text size is not a preference, it is the product

A controlled study measured how long it took people to complete the same task at different font sizes. For adults over 65:

Task completion time by font size, adults 65+ (Yeh, Heliyon 2020)
Font sizeTime
22 pt2.21 s
18 pt5.00 s
14 pt7.04 s
10 pt9.60 s

Younger participants showed no significant difference at all. Small text costs an older adult four times longer and costs a younger designer nothing — which is exactly why it ships.

Now consider that not one vault we examined publishes an accessibility conformance report a buyer can actually read. KeePass states it never will. Two vendors have one locked behind a non-disclosure agreement. And these defects were open in the trackers as we wrote this:

  • Bitwarden on Android: autofill fails when the system font size is increased. The reproduction steps are literally "go to settings and increase the font size by one."
  • Bitwarden on desktop: the NVDA screen reader cannot read the interface.
  • KeePassDX: a TalkBack user cannot reach the title, URL or notes fields.

Increasing the font by one step is the first thing many people do on a new phone. It breaks the core feature.

3. Asking a person for help is slower and less reliable than anyone expects

An 18-month study compared four ways of recovering an account. The result that matters here:

Recovery success by method (Lassak et al., ACM CHI 2024)
MethodSucceededMedian time
Email100%31 seconds
Text message92%52 seconds
Asking a real person64%27 minutes
Security questions57%30 seconds

One in three people who genuinely depended on another human did not get back in. Not because they misunderstood it — because they could not remember who they had chosen, or the person did not reply, or that person's own email was dead.

We should be honest about the limit of this number: that study's participants were mostly aged 18 to 34. Whether a devoted adult child does better than a student's nominated friend has never been measured. It is the single biggest assumption underneath every family vault, including ours.

One thing to stop doing today. Security questions are the most thoroughly discredited mechanism in this entire field. Analysis of millions of real Google account recoveries found 40% of English-speaking US users could not recall their own answers. In a lab study, acquaintances guessed 17% of them. The US standards body now says plainly that services "SHALL NOT" use them. One of our older participants put it better than the papers did: "I do genealogy, no problem finding their mother's names … it's very easy."

What a better design looks like

Three things the evidence points at, in order.

The technical person in the family should do the setup. Every one of these systems dies at the same moment — "install this, now write down these words and keep them safe forever." A study of how people set up shared accounts found the helper usually does it and the credentials are never changed afterwards. If the person who is good at this sits next to the person who is not and does it together, an entire category of abandonment disappears.

Helping someone get back in is a different thing from opening their vault, and they should be separate. Every shipped product conflates them. As one paper put it, emergency access "will typically grant access to all credentials, not a subset." But the common case is not "take over everything" — it is "Mum is locked out and I want to help." That can be a capability someone holds permanently and harmlessly, because it only works with her participation. Opening the vault outright is a different switch, and it should be the deliberate one.

Do not build the clever cryptography. We looked hard at splitting a key into shares among relatives, and at the "guardian" schemes from cryptocurrency wallets. Both fail here. Splitting a key means matched sets of 20-word mnemonics, formats that are not interoperable between tools, and a wrong passphrase that produces not an error but a plausible-looking empty vault. The guardian schemes require each relative to run their own crypto wallet, and the reference implementation's human step is comparing emoji on a phone call with someone claiming to be a relative in distress. The most cited justification for guardians being suitable for non-technical people turns out to cite no study at all.

The problem we have not solved

It would be dishonest to end on the design without this.

The US National Elder Mistreatment Study, covering 5,777 people, found a one-year prevalence of 5.2% for financial mistreatment by family members. Subsequent work found that older adults exploited by family lose four times as much money as those exploited by strangers. And the security research on technology-enabled abuse describes the attacker not as a hacker but as "an adversarial but authenticated user" — someone who was given access legitimately, often while the relationship was good.

The person a family vault is designed to empower is, statistically, the most likely person to abuse it.

We do not think this means don't build it. Families genuinely need this, and the alternative — passwords on paper by the kitchen phone, or shouted down the line — is worse on every axis. But it does mean the design has to prefer notified, revocable, scoped and visible access over silent, permanent, total access, every single time there is a choice. And it means any feature that fires when you cannot object must not rely on you objecting.

That is the constraint we are building against. We would rather say it out loud than discover it in a review.
