The two corrections, first

Correction to R7-a

You ruled that a revised answer should be recoverable from the logs. The logs cannot hold it — and the reason has nothing to do with privacy, so it would have bitten us even if the policy question were settled.

Our deploy config has no logging section. Kamal therefore falls back to Docker's default file driver at max-size=10m, and Docker's max-file defaults to 1. That is a ten-megabyte ring buffer: when it fills, the oldest lines are discarded silently. It also dies with its container, so a redeploy starts a fresh one, and old containers are pruned after five deploys. A revision history that evaporates on the sixth deploy is not a history — it is a way to lose the data slowly enough that nobody notices until they need it.

The good news is that what you actually asked to recover — was it revised, by whom, on which question, when — needs no answer text at all, and I have built that. The question of keeping the original answer itself is R10 below.

Correction to the local-model plan

A local model cannot run on the server. Qwen2.5-7B compressed to four bits is about 4.7 GB of weights. The box has 2 GB of RAM in total, and no swap, shared with MySQL, the proxy and your prayer app. So the failure mode is not "slow" — it is the kernel's out-of-memory killer taking down the website. Even a 1.5B model does not fit.

And on two shared CPU cores it would not be usable if it did: a published study of CPU inference found 7B-class models on one or two threads "frequently led to timeouts, as the context windows could not be filled within 1 minute." That is under two words a second, on the same cores that serve the pages.

What is actually running

Unchanged from Ask #7: one Hetzner CPX11 — 2 vCPU, 2 GB, no swap — carrying quiz.jayteesf.com and prayer.his-service.net behind one proxy. Production still has no LLM key, so every AI button still returns manufactured questions from the offline stand-in. That is the R1 ruling working as designed, but it is worth saying plainly that it is still the state of the live site.

Decision R10

When a student changes their answer, what happens to the first one?

Today the row is overwritten and the original is gone. You asked for it back. Since the logs cannot hold it, the choice is whether to keep it properly in the database or to settle for knowing that a change happened.

Worth knowing what the two open-source products do, because it is the same answer twice: Moodle never overwrites — every submission is a numbered step, shown to teachers as a "Response history" table and hidden from students. Canvas records each answer as an event, visible through a per-course View Log that is off by default. Kahoot locks your first tap; Google Forms overwrites and keeps nothing. So teachers do not universally expect this, and where it exists it is a permissioned database feature.

One constraint that rules out the cheap version of "keep everything": the amended children's-privacy rule (16 CFR 312.10, in force since April) requires a written retention policy and forbids indefinite retention, and four state student-privacy laws require deleting a named student's data on request. You cannot delete one student out of an append-only log file. You can out of a table.

Decision R11

The AI path after copy/paste

You ruled copy/paste as the answer for now, and it ships. The question is what comes next, because "local model" — the follow-up you named — turns out not to be a cost strategy. Here is the arithmetic, with my volume assumption stated so you can attack it: roughly 200 generation calls a month, about 1,500 words in and 1,000 out each.

PathCost to youQuestion quality
Copy/paste (today)$0Whatever model they already pay for — the best on this list
They paste in their own key$0Same, and the button just works
Our key, cheapest tier~$0.10/month; ~$9.50 at 100× the volumeGood, not frontier
Own the hardware$97–163/month, every monthWorse than all of the above

Self-hosting only breaks even somewhere past half a billion words a month. And the prior art is one-sided: Quizlet, Brainscape and Kahoot all put AI generation behind a paid tier, while every Anki add-on asks for the user's own key — one of them notes that "a single dollar can generate thousands of flashcards." Nobody runs local models.

Local support is still worth having as an opt-in for someone who will not send student text to a company, and it is cheaper to offer than it sounds, because the local engines can be forced to emit correctly-shaped output by construction. But it belongs after the two above, not instead of them.

Decision R12

How long do the logs live, now that there are real logs?

The app now writes warnings and errors, which it never did before — that was your R6. But the ten-megabyte ring buffer described above applies to those too, so the new logging is only as useful as its retention. This is a config decision and it is cheap either way; what it really decides is whether a stall reported on Friday can still be investigated on Monday.

Note the box: 40 GB of disk, shared. Log retention is not free, and the children's-privacy rule wants a written retention period rather than "forever."

More than multiple choice

You asked why the AI only writes multiple-choice questions, and whether it could write every kind a card supports so you can pick which to publish. The app has been able to ask four kinds — multiple choice, true/false, typed answer, and fill-in-the-blank — for over a week. Only the writing was narrow.

The useful discovery is what it costs. Three of the four kinds need no AI at all. A typed question is the card — question on the front, answer on the back, nothing to invent. A fill-in-the-blank falls out of blanks the card already contains, or by blanking the answer where it appears. True/false pairs the question with an answer that belongs to a sibling card, and runs it through the same plausibility gates the multiple-choice pipeline already uses. Only multiple choice genuinely needs a model.

So "generate every form this card supports" costs the same per card as generating one multiple-choice question costs today — and on a site whose model is the offline stand-in, those three are not a degraded imitation. They are exactly what a real model would produce, because there is nothing to decide.

One thing worth funding, though, and it is not what you would guess: every card imported so far has an empty list of acceptable alternative answers. So a typed question only settles when a student types the answer exactly. One AI call per card, at authoring time, to write the acceptable variants would convert a grading cost you pay on every student, every time, into a one-off. That is the highest-value model call in this whole feature.

Decision R16

How far past the free forms should this go?

The three free kinds have a plain-but-honest voice. A true/false comes out as the question paired with a candidate answer rather than a flowing sentence; a fill-in-the-blank only works on cards that already have a blank or whose answer appears in the question. A model would smooth both — at the cost of turning a free feature into a metered one, and of the offline stand-in having to fake prose, which is how the bad-questions complaint started.

Not on this page

Built today, no ruling needed

The buttons stop pretending. You asked for this directly today: with no AI model connected, every automatic-generation button is now disabled and says so, and the server refuses those requests outright instead of returning a page full of manufactured questions. The copy/paste path is deliberately untouched and is now the obvious one — it never needed a model, because your assistant is the model.

Two ways a question could end up as its own answer. Neither was what you saw, but both were real. Four generated options that all reword the question could be stored, because the check that catches an option echoing the question only fires when exactly one option does — two or more and it goes quiet. And a card whose back repeats its front imported cleanly, then produced a question whose correct answer was the question. Both refused now, at a layer both the automatic and the paste routes pass through.

Your import bookmark never goes stale again. The 15-minute link was baked into the bookmarklet itself, which is why a new link meant dragging the button to your bookmarks bar again. It now carries no link at all; clicking it asks RecallCoach for a fresh one. The link stays one-time and 15 minutes, exactly as you ruled.

The app can say what went wrong. Every question-generation attempt now leaves a record of which topic, which card, how long it took and how it failed — which is what the stalled worker never left behind. There is a command to read it, and one to group student activity by quiz code.

The TV can render emphasis. The tvOS package shipped and the app is locked to it. Worth knowing: while investigating, we found that markup pasted from your own assistant was being sent to the classroom screen literally, so a **bold** would have appeared with its asterisks in front of a room. It is now stripped on the way out and kept in storage, so switching emphasis on later is a one-line change.
