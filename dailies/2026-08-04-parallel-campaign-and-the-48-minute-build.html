Hey · restructure daily · 4 August 2026 · late evening

The 48-minute build was not the compiler.

It was a sidecar that nobody had to run. A non-authoritative candidate front end, on by default, executing through the tree-walking interpreter, with no timeout — burning 96 minutes of CPU on a build whose authoritative output had finished roughly 80 minutes earlier. Ten parallel lanes worked on this checkout tonight. This is what they measured, and what is still red.

How to read the words in this piece.

Reported means a lane claimed it and the work is not merged. Landed means it is merged into the working tree. Verified means the gate was re-run centrally, by the integrator, tonight. Most of what follows is reported. Where a gate was re-run centrally it says so. Nothing here claims a speedup that nobody measured.

The interesting part: what was actually burning the CPU

On August 4 a cold self-host build sat CPU-bound for more than 48 minutes. The obvious suspects were the ones everybody had already agreed to be suspicious of: the old parser's recursive list copying, or the very large generated self-host pipeline. Both were plausible. Both were wrong.

Sampling the live process named it in ten seconds. The hot command was:

build/heyc tools/hey_frontend_shadow.hey --run -- tools/hey_selfc.hey <program.hir>

That is the candidate front-end shadow. It is deliberately, explicitly, architecturally non-authoritative. Its facts never feed HIR, never feed MIR, never feed linking, and cannot make a build succeed or fail. It exists to be compared against the real front end. It had consumed 96 minutes of CPU without finishing.

A 10-second sample(1) put 100% of stacks inside the tree-walking interpreter at around 120 frames of recursion, with the leaf frames dominated by deep copying:

1498  hey_value_clone          1122  hey_json_object_clone     619  hey_json_array_clone
1130  hey_value_release         953  hey_json_object_release   515  hey_json_array_release
 889  hey_strdup                188  hey_json_object_reserve_exact

And the detail that makes it a story rather than a bug report: the authoritative artifacts had already finished about 80 minutes earlier. The build was, for the great majority of its life, producing nothing anyone needed. The observational sidecar had quietly become the critical path.

Three properties combined to do it, and each one on its own would have been survivable:

  • it defaulted on — HEY_FRONTEND_SHADOW defaulted to '1';
  • it ran interpreted, through the seed tree-walker, on the largest source in the tree;
  • it had no timeout — nothing bounded it, so "slow" and "hung" were indistinguishable from outside.
The lesson, stated plainly.

Anything that is not authoritative must be default-off, bounded, and cheap to skip. Otherwise it does not stay optional — it becomes the thing you are waiting for, and it does so invisibly, because nobody profiles the part of the build that "doesn't matter".

The bounded sidecar — 30 ms

The shadow now advances stage by stage and stops at the first exhausted budget: a byte budget checked before tokenizing (because tokenization is itself quadratic), a token budget checked before parse and names, and a wall-clock budget checked between stages. Every outcome writes a receipt, and a refused source records candidate_stage=deferred_source_budget together with the budget that refused it — so a bound firing can never be mistaken for a pass.

This is not a fix to the copying. It is a fix to the sidecar's willingness to spend time. A process-level timeout is still outstanding: budgets checked between stages cannot interrupt one pathological stage.

The names stage — roughly 17x

The candidate resolver was rebuilding its entire work queue on every step and re-spreading the whole accumulator per node — O(N²) array clones, which is exactly the hey_json_array_clone storm the incident sample showed. Removing the node worklist that deep-copied whole subtrees per reduce step gave a clean A/B on an 861-token input, same binary, only HeyNames.hey swapped:

VariantEnd to endNames stage (total minus the ~19.5 s parse floor)
Previous collector71.9 s~52 s
New resolver22.6 s~3 s

Note the parse-only floor. Roughly 19.5 s of that 22.6 s is not the names stage at all, which is why the honest framing is "~17x on the stage" and not "3x on the pipeline".

The tokenizer — two separate fixes, two very different sizes

These are worth separating, because they are independent terms and conflating them would overstate the first one.

First, a scan early-exit hotfix (~28%). Twelve token scanners each reduced over the range from their starting offset to the end of the file, parked on a sentinel once they had finished, and then kept visiting every remaining character — re-reading an accumulator that carried the whole source text on each ignored step. In the interpreted lane a read of a bound identifier is a deep clone, so each ignored character cost a full copy of the source. Adding an early-exiting fold gave, at the largest measured size (371 bytes): 0.426 s → 0.307 s, and 3,914,102 → 3,595,341 clone calls. About 28% wall clock, about 8% fewer clones. Token output byte-identical, independently confirmed by an unchanged token digest from the identity lane.

Real, and not the transformative win. It removed iteration count, not per-step accumulator size.

Second, a cursor-driven tokenizer (the large win). Replacing the all-offset reduction removed the per-step accumulator size as well. At 3,256 source bytes, against the unmodified baseline:

The counters are exact; the wall-clock figure is noisy, because ten lanes were running on this machine at the time.

The growth order, and a retraction

Earlier in the day this front end was described as cubic. That was an overstatement drawn from two small fixtures, and it is now settled by measurement rather than argument. A 52-cell matrix — three stdlib variants × copy-on-write off/on × eight to ten input sizes from 22 bytes to 14 KB — reports clone calls and copied bytes separately.

Total copied-byte exponent never exceeds 2.20. The accurate statement is that the front end is quadratic overall, with a separable near-cubic scanner term: the parked-scanner behaviour that the early-exit hotfix removed does fit at 2.78, and its share of copied bytes rose monotonically from 2.4% at 22 bytes to 45.3% at 3 KB. It was genuinely on track to dominate. It was not the whole shape.

Separately, the identity lane fitted the full candidate front end at O(tokens1.96) and corrected an assumption that mattered: stage-isolated at 766 tokens, names is about 62% of the cost and tokenization alone is about 26% and already quadratic. A fix confined to the names stage would have left a still-quadratic tokenizer behind.

Three findings that are more useful than the speedups

1. The biggest single lever was not copy-on-write

In the interpreted lane, set NAME = push(NAME, x) hits an O(1) syntactic fast path in the runtime. The natural, idiomatic-looking set NAME = [...NAME, x] is O(n) — 250x fewer clones at n=800 for the first form. A spread that reads like a harmless functional idiom was quietly quadratic, and nothing in the source distinguishes them.

2. for x in <array> deep-clones the collection per iteration

Every traversal over a materialised array is O(n²) in clone calls, in the interpreted lane, today. Hoisting that clone out of the interpreter's for arm is proposed to the runtime lane and has not been made.

3. Clone counts and copied bytes disagree — and both matter

The clone-call exponent reaches linear (0.991) only with the cursor tokenizer and copy-on-write enabled. Neither change alone gets there. But copied bytes stay quadratic in every single cell, because the runtime's clone still deep-copies strings. They are not substitutes for each other, and reporting only one number would have hidden the remaining superlinear term. Refcounting strings is the last one, and it is proposed, not made — every runtime file belongs to a lane that is still working.

The honest cold-build number

With the shadow disabled, building a three-line program —

program
  says 1 + 1
end

— from a clean export of the current commit takes 388,920 ms. Six and a half minutes. That is not a pathological input; it is close to the smallest input that exists. It is the real cold-pipeline cost and it belongs in public alongside the wins, not behind them.

Reader migration: 0 of 213

The scoped reader API is frozen — 21 stable fields each carrying a compatibility reader, an explicitly-owned reader, and a non-returning scoped facade, all generated and drift-checked, with no generated function returning a borrow handle and no raw C pointer exposed. That is real progress on the interface.

But no backend lowers the scoped-borrow intrinsic yet, so not one of the 213 local reader sites can actually move. All 257 escape sites remain explicitly owned, as they must. The count tonight is the same count as this morning: 0 of 213.

Authority cutover: 0%. LLVM-hosted identity: unproven.

The new front end is not authoritative anywhere. Every measurement above is the candidate running beside the real pipeline, not instead of it. And every performance figure in this piece is from the seed tree-walking interpreter — the compiled lanes, Stage-0 and LLVM-direct, are entirely unmeasured for scaling. We do not know whether the accumulator-clone term even exists there.

Forecast

The "authority readiness within 24 hours" line is withdrawn. Tonight's evidence does not support it, and it should not have been published without the identity and migration counts beside it. Cross-lane identity is unproven in three of five lanes, zero of 213 local readers are migrated, and authority cutover is at 0%.

No replacement date is offered here. When one is, it will state its assumptions and be labelled a forecast rather than a promise. The next three things that have to happen are not scheduling questions, they are gating ones: repair the LLVM-hosted build path so two identity lanes can run at all; lower the scoped-borrow consumer in every backend so the 213 sites become migratable; then migrate them and prove zero snapshot clones.

Notes on method, since ten agents shared one checkout

Why a shared working tree makes "pre-existing" a claim requiring proof

One lane reported an LLVM build failure as "pre-existing at HEAD". It was not — HEAD builds fine. The cause was an uncommitted, in-flight edit by a concurrent lane, still being worked on. In a shared tree, "it was already broken" needs bisection-grade evidence before it enters a ledger, or transient breakage becomes durable recorded fact.

The same thing happened in the other direction: a stdlib change briefly made a type check red for a few minutes, another lane sampled exactly that window, and reported it as a standing red it had to route around. It was neither pre-existing nor permanent. It is green now, re-checked centrally.

Why one red gate is not a defect at all

A schema-freshness gate is red because a checked-in JSON file was written by an older Ruby that renders an empty array as [\n\n], while this machine's Ruby renders []. Piped through a normalising JSON formatter the two files are identical. The content never drifted; the formatter did. Diff the content before diagnosing drift.

Why a passing gate is reported with its margin

The front-end scaling acceptance gate was red by design and is now passing at 2.179 against a threshold of 2.20. That margin is 0.021, and the sweep stops at n=16, where the very next doubling ratio is 2.288. It is promoted, and the margin and the sweep limit are recorded on the gate row itself, so the next person to read it cannot mistake it for comfort.

Why three regression scripts are recorded as NOT RUN rather than as passing

Three scripts begin by invoking the build, which one lane was not permitted to do. It ran their contents instead — the underlying contracts and every individual check — and all of those passed. The scripts themselves were recorded as NOT RUN, not as green. That distinction is the whole point of keeping the words separate.
