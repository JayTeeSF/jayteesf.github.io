RESEARCH COMMISSION — the member/key collision: who else has it, and what did it cost them?

**Commissioned by:** the maintainer, 2026-07-29

**Orchestrated by:** the `bench` seat, with a research team

**Deliverable:** markdown research files + a decision recommendation

**Status of the underlying question:** **RULED — 2026-07-29, by the

maintainer, in-session, on a plain-English re-ask he requested.** The

deferral is CLOSED; the article remains wanted as a PUBLICATION, not

as a decision gate. The three questions and today's fourth, with his

answers:

1. **Adopt the exposing-import design? ADOPTED.** `Module.count(obj)`

by default; `obj.count` dot-sugar only via `import Module expose

count`; user keys and user function names always win otherwise.

Re-confirmed explicitly after he challenged the user-wins premise

and heard the full argument both ways.

2. **Scope? UNIFORMLY.** No type exemption (rule 30 as written).

3. **Warning channel? OWN SLICE, and warnings CAN HARDEN into errors**

(per-code promotion).

4. **(New, urgent) The 214-name function-shadow lane divergence:

USER WINS in BOTH lanes, with a MANDATORY WARNING on every

builtin override, suppressible only by an explicit, knowing

opt-out in the code** ("that should be a warning if the user

overrides a built-in at the very least" — verbatim). wfnwins +

wwarnchannel are a coordinated pair.

He also asked whether an interim nobody-wins ERROR is needed purely

to discover update sites; answered NO — a static census over all

system .hey code (running 2026-07-29) finds every site; a flag-gated

strict mode ships WITH wwarnchannel as the dry-run.

Bus records: orchestrator outbox 172929Z, 173531Z, 173929Z.

The material below is the pre-ruling record, kept for provenance.

Interim answer below is superseded by the ruling above.

---

THE MAINTAINER'S INTERIM RULING

> "So far the answer is `get('count')` or its syntactic-sugar-equivalent

> `['count']`."

Both verified working on v0.99.470a, **both lanes agreeing**, so this is

a design question and not a defect:

program
  let h = {count: 3, other: 7}
  says 'dot     : ' + h.count      # 2   <-- the FIELD COUNT, not your value
  says 'get     : ' + get(h, 'count')  # 3
  says 'bracket : ' + h['count']       # 3
  says 'other   : ' + h.other          # 7
end

Interpreter and compiled binary produce identical output. `build_rc=0`,

`run_rc=0`.

**This ruling is a holding position, not a resolution.** Two separate

app teams have now lost time to it. The research below decides whether

we keep it.

---

THE FINDING THAT REFRAMES THE QUESTION

The ticket said "`.count` returns the field count instead of your value."

That is true but it is not the interesting part. The probe above shows

**`h.other` returns 7** — so in Hey, **dot DOES reach your data.** It

reaches your data *except* on a reserved set of builtin names, where the

builtin silently wins.

Compare the two references the maintainer pulled (transcripts verbatim

below). **Neither of them has our problem, and they avoid it in

OPPOSITE directions:**

what `.` means on a plain map/objectcollision possible?
**Ruby**`.` is **never** data. `h2.other` raises `NoMethodError`. Data is `h[:key]`, full stop.**No.** Two syntaxes, two meanings, no overlap.
**JavaScript**`.` is **always** data. `h.length`, `h.size`, `h.keys` are all `undefined` — plain objects have no such members.**No.** Builtins live elsewhere (`Object.keys(h).length`).
**Hey (today)**`.` is data **except** on a shadow list of builtin names, where the builtin wins **silently**.**Yes — and it is silent.**

**This is the thesis to attack or confirm.** Hey may occupy a fourth

position that is worse than either reference, not because either

alternative is obviously right, but because ours is the only one of the

three where a habit the language actively encourages (`h.other` works!)

silently returns something else on a set of names the user cannot see.

A wrong answer with no error is the single worst failure mode we

recognise as a project, and this is one.

**If the research refutes this framing, say so.** A refutation is a

deliverable.

---

SOURCE TRANSCRIPTS — the maintainer's own, verbatim

Three things worth drawing out, and the team should verify each rather

than take my reading:

1. `h.count` → 1 is **Hash#count, the Enumerable method** — it is not a

collision resolution at all, because the data was *never* reachable

by dot in the first place. Line 005 is the load-bearing line.

2. `h[:count]` → 3. Ruby's answer is the same as our interim answer.

3. Lines 006–008: when you attach a **real method** named `count` to a

real object, `o.count` → 3 and there is no ambiguity — because it is

a method, in the method namespace, by construction. Ruby never has

two different kinds of thing competing for one name.

(Note `define_method` is private on `Module` and not available on an

`Object` instance; `define_singleton_method` is the working form.

Incidental to the question, but do not misreport it.)

Node (v23.10.0)

> h = {count: 3}
{ count: 3 }
> h.count
3
> h.length
undefined
> h.len
undefined
> h.size
undefined

`h.count` → **3**: the data wins, cleanly. And the reason there is no

collision is line 3 onward — **plain objects have no `length`, `size`,

or `keys` members at all.** The builtins are static functions

(`Object.keys(h).length`), so they were never in the same namespace.

THE RULE — stated canonically by the maintainer, 2026-07-29. STANDING RULE 30.

> **"dot-methods are a syntactic-sugar (nice-to-have) they only (ever)

> exists because of an explicit import and they lose to conflicting

> names. At which point the system will (generously) produce a warning

> message."**

**This is a rule, not a preference, and it closes most of what this

commission was going to decide.** Quote it; do not paraphrase it.

  • **Sugar, not substance.** `Module.fn(x)` is the real spelling and is

always correct. If a feature works *only* through the dot form, that

is a defect.

  • **Only ever via an explicit import.** No value has default

dot-methods. **UNIFORM ACROSS TYPES — arrays and strings get no

exemption.** And **no implicit prelude / auto-import**: "explicit"

means a line the author wrote.

  • **They lose to conflicting names.** The user's key always wins.
  • **The warning is owed, and generous.** Losing silently is the failure

the rule exists to prevent.

**The orchestrator argued for type-scoping (maps lose default members,

arrays/strings keep theirs) and was overruled.** The reasoning:

type-scoping is cheaper ergonomically but makes users learn a rule

**plus a list of exempt types**. One rule with no exceptions is

teachable in a sentence — the same anti-cruft ruling as standing rule

25, applied to syntax.

**ACCEPTED COST, recorded so nobody rediscovers it as a surprise:**

`[1,2,3].count` and `'hey'.length` require an import too. Real friction

against the Ruby-parity north star. **Quantify it; do not re-litigate

it.**

**WHAT REMAINS FOR THE RESEARCH:** validation and prior art (does

anyone else do this uniformly, and what did it cost them?), the

warning-channel policy questions, the warning site, and the

receiver-split measurement that sizes the migration.

---

THE MAINTAINER'S PROPOSED DESIGN (2026-07-29) — superseded by rule 30 above, kept for its measurements

> "make sure `.count` is only available as the result of a module

> import. And if that module is imported BUT not exposed upon the

> current program then objects shouldn't have a default `count`.

> Meaning you initially get `SomeModule.count(object)`; unless you use

> our new `import SomeModule expose count` at which point you can call

> `object.count`; otherwise there's no confusion with object's keys."

Builtin members stop being **default members of a value**. They arrive

from a module, qualified, and become dot-callable only where explicitly

exposed. **Default state: `obj.count` is always the key.** The ambiguity

does not vanish — it **moves to a line the author wrote**, which a

reader can see and a grep can find.

This is essentially **Rust's trait/`use` design**: a trait method does

not exist on a value until the trait is in scope. It is also not a new

mechanism for us — the exposing-import redesign is already on the board.

THE MEASURED SHADOW LIST (v0.99.470a, probed empirically)

Each name given to a map as a key, then read back through dot:

shadowed (builtin wins)key wins
`count` `size` `length` `keys` `values` `empty` `sort` `reverse` `type` `to_s``first` `last` `min` `max` `sum` `join` `push` `pop` `shift` `class` `name` `each` `map` `filter` `reduce`

**Ten names.** The blast radius is small and the change is tractable.

**SEPARATE DEFECT, found while probing, not part of this design

question:** `h.empty` / `h.sort` / `h.reverse` on a map holding a key of

that name return **`#<stop>`** — an internal sentinel leaking into user

data. Not the key, not the method, not an error. Filed separately.

THE REFINEMENT TO TEST — scope it by type

**The collision exists only for KEYED values.** An array has no user key

named `count`; a string has none named `length` (verified: `arr.count=3`,

`str.length=3`). So scope the rule to maps/objects — *they* get no

default members, arrays/strings/numbers keep theirs. This kills the

ambiguity exactly where it lives and leaves array/string chaining

untouched.

**Research question: does any language scope a rule like this BY TYPE

rather than uniformly? Principled, or a hack that will bite us?**

RULED BY THE MAINTAINER 2026-07-29 — KEY WINS, AND WE SAY SO

> "If they actually name their keys in conflict, the key wins, but at

> least we let them know they lost the `.<method>`"

**This fork is CLOSED.** Once a name is exposed and the map has that

key: **the key wins**, `Enumerable.count(obj)` stays as the unambiguous

form, and **the compiler tells the author they lost the `.method`.**

This resolves the trade cleanly. Key-wins alone would swap a silent

wrong *answer* for a silent non-firing *method* — still silent. **The

warning is what closes that residual silence**, which is why the two

halves of his design are one decision and not two.

**Research this as VALIDATION, not as a live fork:** who else chose

key-wins, what did it cost, and did anyone choosing the other way regret

it? If prior art says key-wins is a mistake, that is a finding worth

bringing back — but the burden has moved. Start from his position.

(Rust's inherent-vs-trait resolution order and Python's `__getattr__`

vs `__getattribute__` remain the comparisons.)

THE COST, to be quantified rather than waved at

Ruby developers **do** type `h.keys` and `h.size` on hashes. Under this

proposal that becomes `Map.keys(h)` or one import line per file. Real

friction. The north star is *equal* ergonomics to Ruby.

A NUMBER NOT TO TRUST

A grep of `examples/`+`stdlib/` for the ten shadowed names returns 168

uses, `.length` alone at 103. **That counts USES, NOT RECEIVERS.**

Whether those are mostly arrays and strings — which would make migration

nearly free — is **UNMEASURED**, and it is the number that decides the

cost of the entire proposal. **Getting the receiver split is a concrete

deliverable and probably the highest-value single measurement here.**

---

THE MAINTAINER'S SECOND ASK (2026-07-29) — a compile-time conflict warning

> "what if we ALSO have syntax warnings to let the user know they named

> their attribute in-conflict with an imported method? (assuming that

> would be easy to track and not slow down our language) — can it be a

> compile-time thing vs. runtime (so we don't slow down runtime)"

**Answer: yes, compile-time, RUNTIME COST EXACTLY ZERO** — nothing is

added to emitted code. The check is a hash lookup per *literal* key

against the file's exposed-name set at parse/HIR time.

statically knowableNOT knowable
`{count: 3}` literal keys; `put(h, 'count', v)` literal string keys`put(h, k, v)` variable key; JSON parsed at runtime; keys from another module

Partial coverage — but literal keys are where people actually *write* a

field named `count`.

MEASURED, so nobody re-derives it

  • `heyc <file> --check` **exists**, silent on success, rc=0. Natural home.
  • `warn` is a **RUNTIME STATEMENT** in the language (verified: prints to

stderr, continues). **Unrelated to compiler diagnostics — do not

conflate them.**

  • **ZERO compiler warning sites. Our compiler is ERRORS-ONLY today.**

THEREFORE THE REAL COST IS THE CHANNEL, NOT THE CHECK

This introduces **compiler warnings as a concept**, which we have never

had. Policy rides along: suppressible and how; does `--check` fail CI on

a warning; do warnings ever harden into errors; is the output

machine-readable. **Research the three poles:** Rust's lint levels

(`allow`/`warn`/`deny`/`forbid` + `#[allow]`), **Go's deliberate refusal

to have warnings at all** (a real, defensible position — take it

seriously rather than assuming warnings are good), and Clang/Swift's

`-Wno-` granularity.

THE CHANNEL PAYS FOR ITSELF THREE TIMES — cost this

Three pending items already want it and cannot have it:

1. `Web.rate_limit` / `Web.cors`, still declared and **silently inert**

(the app team's ask 1, the most valuable of their three);

2. the three approved renames, which want **deprecation** warnings

rather than hard breaks;

3. this conflict warning.

If the evidence supports it, recommend the channel as **its own slice on

its own merits**, not a rider on the member/key decision.

THE CAVEAT THAT WILL BE ENFORCED ON ANY DESIGN

**Partial coverage that reads as total.** Ship "we warn you about

conflicts" and users will believe they are covered, then get bitten by

the JSON-parsed case. **Structurally identical to "TSAN CLEAN" meaning

"no races on the paths a spec happens to drive"** — our eleventh

instrument-blindness finding, from this week. So:

  • (a) the warning **text must state its own bound** — *"no conflicts

among literal keys"*, never *"no conflicts"*;

  • (b) the warning **must not be presented as the safety mechanism**.

Research whether **any** language's diagnostics state their own coverage

bound. If almost none do, this is a small original contribution rather

than a copy — and worth saying so in the article.

THE DIAGNOSTIC — the maintainer's framing, which is better than mine

I had specified *"key `count` conflicts with exposed method `count`"* —

neutral, and it never says **what happened**. His framing is that we

tell them **they lost the `.method`**. A diagnostic should state what

the compiler DID and how to get the other thing back:

warning: this key shadows the exposed method `count`
  --> app.hey:12
   |
12 |   let h = {count: 3}
   |            ^^^^^  your key wins here — `h.count` reads 3
   |
   = `count` was exposed at app.hey:1 (`import Enumerable expose count`)
   = to call the method instead, write `Enumerable.count(h)`
   = this check covers LITERAL keys only; keys built at runtime or
     parsed from JSON are not checked

The last line is the self-bounding required above. **Check whether ANY

language's diagnostic states its own coverage bound like that** — if

almost none do, say so; it is a small original contribution.

THE WARNING IS SELF-LIMITING — a property of his design, not mine

It can **only fire in a file that wrote `expose count`.** A file that

never opted in has no conflict and never sees it. This matters because

**the normal death of a warning is firing so often that people tune it

out** — and here the population of files that can trigger it is exactly

the population that asked for the method. Verify this reasoning; if it

holds it is a real argument in the design's favour and belongs in the

article.

A SECOND WARNING SITE, specified by neither of us — research it

Warn at the **access site** (`h.count`), not only the key **definition**

site. The definition site is where the mistake is made; **the access

site is where the confusion happens.** Statically knowable when the

receiver is a local with an inferred literal shape; **not** knowable for

a parameter or a runtime-built map — so best-effort where the

definition-site check is reliable. Recommend whether it earns its

complexity.

WHY IT FITS THE PROPOSAL

Under key-wins, a conflict stops being a wrong **answer** and becomes a

method that **does not fire** — benign but silent. A compile-time

warning is exactly the right instrument for benign-silent. The two

compose: **the default buys correctness, the warning buys back

discoverability.** Check that reasoning; refute it if it is wrong.

---

WHAT TO RESEARCH — the maintainer's questions, in his words

> "How have they resolved it? Does any other language have this issue?

> (why / why not)"

Take the second question as seriously as the first. **"Why not" is the

more useful finding**, because a language that structurally cannot have

this problem is showing us a design, not a workaround.

Structural axes — classify each language, do not just narrate it

1. **One namespace or two?** Are fields/keys and methods in the *same*

name space (collision possible) or *separate* ones (collision

impossible)? Rust and Java put fields and methods in separate

namespaces — `s.count` and `s.count()` can both be legal and mean

different things. Verify this; it is a real escape route and we have

not considered it.

2. **Syntactic distinction.** Lua's `t.k` is exactly `t["k"]` while

methods are called with a colon (`t:m()`) through a metatable. Does

the punctuation itself resolve it?

3. **No methods on collections at all.** Elixir (`Map.size(m)`),

Clojure (`(count m)`), PHP (`count($a)`), Go — if the collection has

no methods, there is nothing to collide with. **This is the most

structurally clean answer and the team should cost it honestly for

Hey**, including what it would break.

4. **Data wins + escape hatch** (JS, Groovy).

5. **Method/builtin wins + subscript escape** (this is *us*, and

nominally Ruby — but see above: Ruby is not really in this bucket).

6. **Compile-time error on collision.** Go rejects a struct with a

field and method of the same name. C# similar. **Would a compile-time

or load-time diagnostic work for us?** We cannot always know keys

statically, but we may know them often enough for a warning to pay.

Languages to cover at minimum

Ruby, JavaScript, **Groovy** (likely our closest living analog — dot on

maps reaches data *and* maps have methods; find out what that cost

them), Python (`dict` has no attribute access; but look at

`SimpleNamespace`, `dataclass`, `__getattr__`, and pandas

`df.count` vs `df['count']` — **pandas is a direct, well-documented,

much-complained-about instance of exactly our problem and may be the

single most valuable case study**), Lua, PHP, Perl, Go, Rust, Java, C#,

Swift, Kotlin, Elixir, Clojure, Nix, Jsonnet, and template languages

(Jinja2, Liquid, Handlebars) where `{{ item.count }}` has this problem

acutely.

Specific things to hunt for — evidence, not opinion

  • **A CVE or security-incident class caused by this resolution.**

JavaScript's "own properties shadow the prototype" rule is the root of

**prototype pollution**, and `Object.create(null)` / `Map` exist

partly because of it. Confirm or refute; if confirmed it is the

strongest argument in the whole report, because it turns a taste

question into a safety question.

  • **Languages that CHANGED their answer**, and what the migration cost.

A language that moved is worth ten that never had the problem.

  • **Deprecation and escape-hatch design**: when a language has to let

you say "I mean the data", what does that look like, and did users

actually adopt it?

  • **The reverse hazard**: does any language let user data shadow a

builtin and thereby *break* the builtin? (JS: `{toString: 1}` —

check what that does to string coercion.) Our current design is

immune to this, which is a point in its favour, and the report must

say so rather than arguing one side.

---

RULES FOR THIS RESEARCH

1. **Run the code.** Every claim about a language's behaviour must come

with a transcript from an actual interpreter or compiler, with the

version recorded, exactly as the maintainer supplied for Ruby and

Node. **No behaviour asserted from memory or from documentation

alone.** We have been bitten eleven times this week by instruments

that reported without measuring; a research report that cites docs

instead of a REPL is the same failure in a new costume.

2. Where you cannot run it, **say so explicitly and mark the claim

UNVERIFIED**. An honest gap beats a confident guess.

3. **Ruby gets its own section regardless of what the survey finds** —

the north star is that a Ruby developer must find Hey's ergonomics

equal. Whatever we choose must be defensible to someone who types

`h[:count]` without thinking about it.
