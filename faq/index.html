Top5 — a party game about being right about people

How Top5 works

Everybody writes an ordered five on one topic. Then something decides the real order, and whoever’s list came closest wins. Order is the whole game.

Four ways to decide the real order

You choose before anybody writes, because it changes what you would write. Each walkthrough below is a real game, recorded from the actual phone screen and the actual television screen side by side.

The room decides

“the room, out loud”

The party version. Everybody’s number ones go on the table at once, the room votes, and the winner takes slot one of the real order. Then slot two, and so on. It takes the longest and it is the one people shout during.

Pick this when the arguing is the evening.

Your lists, added up

“the room, added up”

Nobody votes. The five private lists are added together — your number one is worth 5, your number five is worth 1 — and whatever order falls out is the answer. It is the same people as “the room”, asked before anybody spoke.

Pick this when the room will not stop talking, or when you want a game finished in five minutes.

The numbers decide

“the numbers”

Not an opinion at all. Every item the room named is looked up in a table of gathered figures — records sold, box office, chart weeks — and ranked by it. It still only ranks what your room actually wrote down: there is no answer key.

Pick this on topics with real numbers behind them. The gap between what a room believes and what is measurably so is the entire point.

Everybody who played

“everybody who played this”

Your items, ranked by how every previous room on this topic actually placed them. The same question as “your lists”, asked of everybody instead of the five people in the room.

It needs five finished games on the topic before it will say anything. Under that it tells you how many more it needs rather than inventing an order.

Questions

What is Top5, in one sentence?

Everybody writes an ordered five on one topic. Then something decides the real order, and whoever’s list came closest wins.

What do I need to play?

A phone, and somebody to start a game. Players never install anything — you open a link or scan a code and you are in.

The Apple TV app is optional and belongs to the room, not to the players: it is installed once by whoever owns the living room, the same commitment as owning the board game. The game is fully playable with phones alone.

How is it scored?

Once a slot of the real order is known:

  • Your item, in that same slot — that slot’s weight: 5, 4, 3, 2, 1.
  • Your item, somewhere else on your list — 1.
  • You did not have it — nothing.

So a perfect list is 15, and putting something first is a bet: worth five if the real order agrees with you, worth one if it merely shows up.

Why are there four ways to decide the order?

Because they disagree, and the disagreement is the interesting part. A room shouting about the best pop act of the eighties and a table of certified sales figures are answering different questions, and watching them diverge is better than either on its own.

You pick before anybody writes, because it changes what you would write. The list you would offer a room is not the list you would offer the charts.

Is there a right answer?

No, and that is deliberate. There is no answer key anywhere in the game. The items the players wrote down are the only candidates there are, and a judge’s whole job is to put those in order. An item nobody in the room named cannot win a slot, however famous it is.

What is the “why” line?

One optional line on why you picked something, captured while you are picking it. It is what turns a ranking into a recommendation, and you only ever get it in the moment — ask afterwards and nobody remembers.

What happens to my list?

Nobody sees your picks until they are on the table, including the television. The big screen never shows a player anything their own phone should be showing them.

Finished games feed a per-topic board — what rooms actually rank first — and that board records no player, no room and no individual list. Aggregate is public; individual is not. A board is “rooms ranked these”, never “this person ranked these”.

Where do the numbers come from?

They are gathered offline, per topic, and the table always carries its source. The server never fetches anything while you play.

A topic nobody has gathered numbers for is refused rather than guessed. A ranking presented as “the numbers” with nothing behind it is an opinion in a lab coat.

Why does the board need five games?

Because a board built from four games is a lie. Under five finished games on a topic, the board is not published and the “everybody” judge will not run — it tells you how many more games it needs instead.

How many people?

Two is enough, twelve is the cap, and three or four is where it is best. Everybody votes at once, so nobody waits their turn.
