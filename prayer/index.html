How to turn a Psalm into your own prayer.

The Lord's Prayer teaches the pattern. The Psalms give us words—and the freedom to be honest. Here is a way to move from reading a verse to speaking to the Father.

✦   START WITH PSALM 23

Four small moves. No special words.

Read slowly. Ask what the passage reveals about God, then let it shape what you bring to him today.

  1. 01
    Read the Scripture.

    Read Psalm 23 all the way through. Notice its movement from green pastures to a dark valley—and God's presence in both.

  2. 02
    Notice what is true of God.

    God is pictured as a shepherd who guides, restores, provides, and remains close. Begin with his character, not simply a list of requests.

  3. 03
    Speak honestly about your life.

    Where are you following fear instead of his guidance? Where do you need rest, wisdom, or courage? Put that into your own words.

  4. 04
    Remember others.

    Jesus taught us to pray our Father. Include someone else who needs a shepherd's care, then rest quietly before God.

THE CONNECTION TO JESUS' MODEL

Our Father: trust his care. Hallowed be your name: honor the Shepherd's character. Your will be done: follow his paths. Daily bread: depend on his provision. Deliver us: ask for courage in the valley. Our / us: remember other people.

AN EXAMPLE PRAYER IN MY OWN WORDS
Father, thank you for being my shepherd. Show me where I am following fear instead of your guidance. Help me recognize the places where you are providing rest. Teach me to walk in paths that honor your name, and help me care for others who are struggling to find their way.

The pattern Jesus gave his disciples

The Lord's Prayer is a way of life.

Read Matthew 6:5–15 ↗ and Luke 11:1–13 ↗. These seven movements are a practical way to learn the prayer; traditions also count six or seven petitions.

Five habits underneath the pattern

  • God first: God's name, kingdom, and will precede requests for provision.
  • Relationship, not performance: the Father knows our needs; prayer isn't a performance or a formula for forcing outcomes (Matthew 6:5–8).
  • Our, us, we: even private prayer includes our neighbors and the wider world.
  • Prayer moves us to action: to ask for God's will and forgiveness is to seek obedience and reconciliation.
  • Bold trust without control: ask honestly, persist faithfully, and trust God's goodness (Luke 11:9–13).
Read the model prayer (King James Version)

Our Father which art in heaven, Hallowed be thy name.
Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven.
Give us this day our daily bread.
And forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors.
And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil:
For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever. Amen.

The closing doxology is familiar from later Greek manuscript tradition and Christian worship; it is absent from some of the earliest witnesses to Matthew. Compare 1 Chronicles 29:10–13.

What the prayers teach together

Connections worth returning to.

Put Scripture beside Scripture. These pairings reveal how Jesus' model gathers together the vocabulary and concerns of the whole Bible.

THE GREEK SCRIPTURES

The Septuagint (LXX)

The Greek translation of the Jewish Scriptures began centuries before Jesus and influenced Greek-speaking Jewish communities and New Testament authors. We cannot presume every disciple personally read the LXX; Scripture was also heard and learned through Hebrew and Aramaic traditions. Psalm numbers sometimes differ: Psalms 34, 23, and 27 in many English Bibles are 33, 22, and 26 in the Greek Psalter.

JEWISH PRAYER TRADITION

God's name and kingdom

The Kaddish and Amidah illuminate shared Jewish themes of sanctifying God's name, longing for his reign, and seeking his will. Similarities with Jesus' prayer are meaningful, but do not demonstrate that he copied the surviving wording of these prayers, which developed over time.

THE EARLY CHURCH

A prayer worth repeating

The early Christian Didache (chapter 8) preserves a version of the prayer and directs believers to pray it three times a day. Jesus criticized empty repetition, not thoughtful, persistent prayer. His own prayers drew on the Psalms, including Psalms 22 and 31 during his suffering.
