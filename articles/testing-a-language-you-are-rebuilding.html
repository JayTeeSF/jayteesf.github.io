The Answer Key Cannot Be One of the Contestants

Rebuilding a language creates a problem that is not specific to us. The new implementation cannot mark its own homework. The old one can — but it is what you are replacing, and grading against it pins its bugs in place along with its behaviour. You need an answer key that belongs to neither. Here is the one we chose, the three we rejected after measuring them, and everything it can never tell us.

Status: designed and measured, not yet built

Every measurement below was taken. The checker itself is four-fifths built; the missing fifth — actually running the outside language instead of typing its answers in by hand — has not shipped.

TL;DR

  • The answer key is a boring language that was already installed. A shell script runs our programs; Ruby computes what the answers should be. It does not define what our language means — where we chose differently, it is not asked. Of the 224 specs that exercise how our language behaves, 110 (49%) are checkable this way today.
  • Three candidates were eliminated by measurement, not taste. One has no decimals. One is decimal only. One answers 2 × (263 − 1) as 18446744073709551616 — two too high — and exits reporting success. A silent wrong answer inside the instrument.
  • The missing piece is that the outside column is hand-typed. The reference language is never actually run. Re-measuring reproduced 86 of 87 rows exactly, so this is cheap insurance, not a fire — but a transcription is not a measurement.
  • Disclosed weakness: the range is narrow. Nothing about speed, nothing about compilation, nothing about the order messages arrive in, nothing where we ruled against it. An answer key trusted outside its range is worse than none.
  • Audit your own checker in one line. Ask it something whose answer you know independently, and read the exit code too: awk 'BEGIN { print 2*(2^63-1) }'.

Neither of our own implementations can grade the rebuild

Two lanes, one language

Our language runs your program two ways. The interpreter walks through it doing what it says — that is also what answers you at the prompt. The compiled lane turns it into a binary first. They must agree on every answer, and when they do not, nothing inside the project can say which is wrong. The other three documents in this set use those same two words.

The interpreter has been our answer key, because it is simpler and older. That is a habit, not a justification. Our arithmetic table records 40 rows where the lanes disagree, and the outside answers already sitting in that file say which lane is broken in 33 of the 40 (82.5%). In 29 it is the compiled binary — mostly rows where it drops the fractional part of a decimal and answers 2.5 * 2 with 4. In four it is the interpreter.

The first three trace to one character. To decide whether a minus sign means subtraction or a negative number, each lane scans backwards over a list of characters to skip. The interpreter's list includes a closing bracket. The compiler's does not. It survived because the only thing positioned to catch it was the thing with the bug. A wrong answer in the answer key is the worst place for one.

Three obvious checkers failed the entrance exam

The job splits in two. Driving — run the program, capture the output and the exit code, compare — is process wrangling, and a shell script already does it well. Computing what the answer should be needs arithmetic you can trust.

We measured five candidates; three are unusable. The shell has no decimals at all — 2.5 * 2 is a syntax error — and 30 of our 40 lane disagreements are about decimal behaviour. It also wraps at the 64-bit integer boundary, which is one of the five behaviours this exercise exists to settle. A checker that shares your problem is not a second opinion. It is the same opinion twice.

awk fails worse, and this is the one to remember. The shell refuses loudly. awk answers: 18446744073709551616 where the truth is 18446744073709551614, and it exits reporting success. A silent wrong answer inside the instrument is the failure class this project keeps paying for.

bc is subtler still: it fails by being better than we need. Its arbitrary-precision decimal arithmetic says 0.1 + 0.2 is .3. In the binary floating-point every mainstream language actually performs, the answer genuinely is 0.30000000000000004. bc would report our correct answer as a defect.

Ruby 4.0.6 and Python 3.14.6 both answer all three correctly. We picked Ruby on one criterion, deliberately unromantic: it is already installed in our Linux build image and Python is not. A checker that is not on the machine does not run. That line in the build recipe is left over from an older bootstrap we have since retired, so it is one tidy-up commit away from not being true — whoever builds this has to pin Ruby on purpose rather than inherit it.

The checker is allowed to disagree with us — that is a switch, not a problem

Pick your checker by how closely it agrees with your language and you have made agreement the goal — and your language can no longer differ from anything on purpose. We decided that (−7) / 2 is −3: division rounds toward zero. Ruby says −4. Under a “pick the one that matches” rule you would swap checkers over one operator's sign convention. A disagreement is not a reason to change checkers. It is a reason to stop consulting the checker for that row. Our table already has the mechanism — a dash meaning not meaningful here — and 8 of its 95 rows use it.

The real danger is the opposite mistake: quietly teaching the checker to agree. One of our programs ends with says 'Count: ' + answer and prints Count: 42. Ruby raises a type error; it will not glue a number onto a string. The obvious Ruby side is a small program with a print helper, and the obvious helper converts the number to text first. Add that and the checker reproduces every line perfectly, and a reader concludes Ruby agrees with us. Ruby does not agree. Ruby refuses. The conversion was our own opinion wearing Ruby's clothes.

The rule that keeps the checker honest

The checker's setup may bind names and forward to operators. It may not compute. A helper defined as a / b forwards, so the row honestly shows Ruby's disagreement. A helper encoding our rounding rule would make the checker agree by construction — a test turned into a tautology.

With about twenty lines of forwarding setup, all 95 rows are legal Ruby, 39 already run unchanged, and 89 of 95 run once that setup binds the six named starting states our own rows share. The other six raise an exception, which is itself a fine recorded answer.

Half the language, and we name which half

Two honest denominators, and we quote both, because quoting only the flattering one is how a coverage claim becomes a lie. Against every file in the language-spec directory, 110 of 760 is 14.5% — the number someone will check. But 408 of those files only read our own source code, and 121 are about compilation, where no outside language has an opinion. Against the specs that genuinely exercise the language it is 110 of 224, or 49%.

A caution about that 760, because this project's most expensive recurring defect is a restated number drifting from the thing it restates. 760 is one directory — the language specs. It is not the whole spec tree, which is 988 files; and it is not the population behind the falsifiability audit further down, which is 964 — the language specs plus the compiler specs. Worse, a live count of that same one directory on a later day returned 773, not 760. Nobody has reconciled the three, and the architecture page records the discrepancy rather than resolving it. Quote none of these fractions without naming the directory it is over.

All 95 rows cost 3.0 seconds, which is cheap enough to run on every commit — and it should be, because its whole value is telling you which lane you just broke.

What it can never tell us

This list sits near the top of the design document too, because an answer key trusted outside its range does more harm than none.

  • Performance. Nothing at all. Ruby cannot tell you our language is fast.
  • Compilation. Nothing. The checker confirms the answer, never the reason. Those 121 specs must be excluded by name, because many look ordinary — they check that the answer is 42 — and would otherwise be swept in as checkable.
  • The order messages arrive in. It can compute “after 1000 messages the counter reads 1000”. It cannot compute an interleaving.
  • Our own error text. It can confirm that something failed, never what we said about it.
  • Anything we ruled on differently — the do-not-consult list above.

One soft boundary bites hardest. Every helper you write on the checker's side is a place the checker stopped being independent. The 110 checkable specs call at most 85 distinct built-in functions — a static count, and a ceiling; the true figure is probably 60 to 70. Write a helper for each and you have a second implementation of our standard library, in Ruby, that nobody tests. So count helpers per row and cap it. And the checker must fail loudly when Ruby is missing, never skip — a skipped checker prints green over an unmeasured table.

Proving a message did not arrive, without ever sleeping

Asserting a message did arrive is easy: wait for it. Asserting it did not and will not arrive is where every concurrency suite turns flaky, because the obvious implementation is sleep(100ms) then check the mailbox is empty — a test that fails on a loaded machine and passes on a fast one. (A mailbox is the queue of messages waiting for one independent unit of work — an actor — to pick up.) Erlang's answer, throughout the Erlang/OTP test suites: send yourself a marker after the operation under test, block until the marker arrives, then look for the other message with a timeout of zero. 

send self(), 'BARRIER'   # sent AFTER the thing being tested
receive m                # blocks until the barrier comes back
if m != 'BARRIER'        # anything ahead of it in the queue is a violation
  says 'LEAK: ' + m
end

The zero timeout is not a race. Both sends came from the same sender to the same destination, and the runtime guarantees such messages keep their order, so the marker cannot overtake the message under test. Once the marker is in your hand the question is already settled. No sleep, no window, no flake.

Now the part worth pausing on. The most battle-tested actor runtime in the world guarantees almost nothing about message order, and says so in one sentence: if A sends a signal to B and later sends another to B, the first is guaranteed not to arrive after the second. That is the whole promise. Per sender-receiver pair, one direction, and not even a promise that the first message arrives. Every ordering test in those suites stands on that one narrow sentence — specify the smallest guarantee you can defend, then build the test vocabulary on it. The marker proves ordering only from the same sender; to prove a third actor sent you nothing, route a marker through that actor.

And can our own tests fail at all?

None of this matters if the checks are inert, so we audited that too: all 276 gate scripts and 964 specs — a different population from the 760 above, being the language specs plus the compiler specs. The house discipline is to delete the fix and confirm the test goes red again — a whole article of its own — documented in 33.8% of defect-fix commits, high precisely because it is a written rule. The step nobody writes down is confirming a new test failed for the reason you expected: about 10.5% of those commits, and the “expected reason” half zero times.

The bigger finding was duller. 215 of 276 checks (78%) are not run by any automated lane, and 51 have no way to be run at all. A check that never runs cannot fail. And of the 159 checks that print a summary line, 83 make at least three claims the check never measured — the summary is a hard-coded string.

Three things worth stealing

Audit the instrument before you trust it. Three of five candidates fell to arithmetic we already knew the answer to, in an afternoon.

Let your checker disagree, and record where. A disagreement list is a per-row switch, not a ranking input. Shim your checker to make disagreements go away and you have replaced a test with a mirror.

Publish the range. Ours is 49% of the specs describing how the language behaves and nothing at all outside that. Less impressive than a vague claim of thorough testing, and far more useful: everybody now knows which parts have no answer key.
