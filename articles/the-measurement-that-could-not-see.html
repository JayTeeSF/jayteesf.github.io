The Measurement That Could Not See the Thing It Was Measuring

We told you our web server beat Go on latency. That claim survives correction at the median, becomes a tie at p99, and at the far tail produced a number we are not yet willing to call — two runs, disagreeing by 3.2x.

TL;DR

  • We are correcting our own latency claim. Measured open-loop at an offered 60,000 requests per second, Hey's median still beats Go — 642 vs 738 µs (microseconds). But the p99 win is gone (1,686 vs 1,612 µs, 4.6% apart: a tie), and at p99.9 Hey measured 2.4x worse (22,874 vs 9,716 µs).
  • The cause was our instrument, not our server. Our load generator was closed-loop: it sends a request only when an earlier one finishes, so when the server stalls the client stalls too and the stall never reaches the results. That failure has a name — coordinated omission (Gil Tene, “How NOT to Measure Latency”, QCon San Francisco 2015).
  • The prediction came before the measurement. A literature review commissioned for an unrelated reason predicted this, on these numbers, and named the three conditions it requires.
  • Disclosed weakness: the p99.9 row is a lead, not a finding. Two repetitions per lane, and Hey's two disagree by 3.2x (34,853 and 10,896 µs). We print the values, not a verdict; more repetitions on a quiet machine are owed before anyone cites that number, us included.
  • Check it yourself in ten seconds: run oha --help and read the --latency-correction line. The correction is ignored unless you also pass -q, a fixed request rate: the fix ships in the tool and does nothing until you change the experiment.

What we said before was the instrument talking

For months this site published latency figures showing our server ahead of Go — p50 65 µs against 233, 121–122 against 136–137, and two more pairs in the certification table. Every one of them came from a closed-loop generator with no target rate and no latency correction. As of today all of them are withdrawn, in a correction block on that article. This piece is about how we found out, and what the corrected measurement says.

A closed-loop client stops asking exactly when you stall

A closed-loop load generator holds a fixed number of requests in flight — ours held 32 — and issues the next only when a response comes back. It is the most considerate customer you will ever have: the moment you get busy, it stops asking.

That politeness is the bug. Suppose the server stalls for ten milliseconds. Real traffic does not pause out of courtesy; arrivals keep coming, queue behind the stall, and wear it in their response times. Those are the samples a tail percentile exists to report, and a closed-loop client sends none of them: it records 32 slow responses instead of the hundreds that should have piled up, then takes percentiles over what it sent. The stall was real, users would have felt it, and the graph is clean.

This is coordinated omission, named and scoped by Gil Tene in “How NOT to Measure Latency” (QCon San Francisco, 2015) and corrected in the HdrHistogram project by recordValueWithExpectedInterval. It is a sibling of, not the same as, the older result that a bounded-concurrency generator can be its own throughput ceiling — Schroeder, Wierman and Harchol-Balter, “Open Versus Closed: A Cautionary Tale”, NSDI 2006. One is about capacity, the other about statistics.

Why we were looking, and what the looking missed

The review that predicted this was not about latency. We had spent a week accumulating nine separate failures of our own measurement tools — not of the software being measured, of the tools — and commissioned a literature search to find whether any of them had names. Four did, and had for decades: a gate that passes without being able to fail is vacuity (Beer, Ben-David, Eisner & Rodeh, CAV 1997); a timed loop the compiler deletes because its result goes unused is dead-code elimination, which the Java Microbenchmark Harness ships a Blackhole object specifically to prevent. Two of the nine we could find no name for at all.

The search then predicted a tenth, in the harness we were using to check the other nine, and it was right. That is the flattering half, so here is the other one. The closed-loop cells were published and stood for months. Nobody inside the team caught them. It took an outside literature review, commissioned about something else entirely, to notice that our latency numbers could not see the thing they were named after — and the review only got commissioned because we had already made nine other mistakes worth writing down. This was not vigilance. It was a lucky consequence of keeping receipts.

What changed when the bias was removed

p99.922,874 µs9,716 µsLead only: Hey 2.4x worse, n=2

The median claim survives, as the review predicted: the bias hit both sides equally, so a real difference comes through it. The p99 claim does not — our old numbers implied a Hey win there, and that implication is withdrawn. And p99.9 is the number the old harness could not see at all, on any day.

The tail row is a lead, not a finding

Hey's two repetitions at p99.9 were 34,853 µs and 10,896 µs — a 3.2x spread. Go's were 10,849 and 8,584, a 1.26x spread. An average of two samples that disagree by 3.2x summarises nothing, and two runs support a direction at best. The table prints the mean because the row must hold a value; this paragraph tells you not to quote it.

One obvious suspect: other builds started running on the same machine partway through the sweep (none at the pre-run check, two at the post-run check). We checked rather than assumed. If that drove the tail, the later repetitions should be the worse ones — but Hey's worst p99.9 is its first, taken on the quiet end. So it does not explain the instability, and is not exonerated either. What is owed before any tail verdict, ours first, is more repetitions on a quiet box. The p50 and p99 rows can correct the record. The p99.9 row is a lead.

The fix was a different experiment, not a flag

This part generalises past us. Our generator, oha, ships a --latency-correction option, and its help text states the catch in one sentence: the correction is ignored unless -q, a fixed request rate, is also set. The flag alone changes nothing and reports no error — with no target rate there is no intended send time to correct against.

So the fix is not a flag; it is a different experiment. A closed-loop run asks “how fast can this server go if I push as hard as it will accept?” A corrected run asks “at 60,000 requests per second arriving whether you are ready or not, what do users experience?” Different questions, different answers — which is why our new 642 µs median and our old 231 µs median are neither a regression nor comparable (different question, and different builds — 467a against 468a), and no longer share a table. A defensible tail number means offering a rate and voiding the run if it is missed.

What you can take to your own harness

Three questions. Does your load generator wait for a response before sending the next request? Do the real arrivals it models wait for you? Do your percentiles correct for missed send times? Yes, no, no means your tail is optimistic by an unmeasured amount — and that error is exactly the size of the stalls you most wanted to know about. The cheaper second lesson: a correction that silently does nothing without a companion setting looks, in a log file, precisely like one that worked.
