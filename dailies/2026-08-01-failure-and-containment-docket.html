When the compiler assumes a number and the data says otherwise

You asked a failure question and I answered a different one. This is the question you actually asked, with the choices as the headers.

Already settled — not up for decision here
  • No visible types. Stdlib-only types are unfair to programmers who avoid them, and that unfairness becomes a feature request.
  • Conversion-directed hints. The compiler names the site and the to_i/to_s that unlocks it; an optimizer may insert them. The hint is a value, never a type.
  • Correctness is non-negotiable. Incorrect programs are unacceptable; that constraint outranks speed.
  • Direction of travel: one implementation, C retained for stage0 and stubbed to warn elsewhere.

The thing I got wrong, stated plainly

I framed the choice as “compile-time only or deopt.” That was not your question. Your question was: the compiler decided this slot holds a number, runtime hands it something else — what happens? Deoptimization is only one answer, it is the one you already rejected, and the interesting answers are the other three.

One clarification that shrinks the problem: if the compiler only specializes where it can prove the type, bogus data cannot arrive by construction. The question is live only at the trust boundary — JSON, stdin, the network, FFI, anywhere a value enters unproven. That is where the decision below applies, and nowhere else. 

let raw = Json.parse(body)     # unproven: could be anything
let n   = to_i(raw['count'])   # <- THE BOUNDARY. what happens if it is "abc"?
loop n times ...               # proven i64 from here on; no checks inside

What deoptimization actually is, and how it compares to your brainstorm

I owe you a correction: you said you did not want complex backtracking and asked what happens when the data is wrong. I recorded that as “deopt rejected.” That was my compression, not your ruling. Here is the real comparison.

Deoptimization is not a correctness risk — it is a correctness mechanism. The compiler guesses a type it cannot prove and emits fast code behind a guard. If the guess is wrong, control transfers into a generic version of the same code, which produces the right answer more slowly. It never crashes and never computes something false. That is the opposite of the thing you are worried about.

The real cost of deopt here is not the guard, it is the bail-out. To leave a specialized region the runtime must materialize the generic state — turn an unboxed i64 buffer back into a boxed array, which means allocating and copying at exactly the moment you were trying to avoid allocating. Occasional bail-outs are cheap. A loop that deopts every iteration is slower than never having specialized.

Why the hybrid is probably right

Your to_i hint mechanism and deopt solve different halves of the same problem. Proof gives you guard-free speed where the compiler can see the answer. Speculation gives you speed where it cannot — JSON in, polymorphic collections, anything crossing a dynamic boundary. And the diagnostic you asked for gets strictly better: instead of only saying “this is slow, add a to_i,” it can say “this is speculated and bailing out 40% of the time — a to_i here makes it proven.” That is a better instrument than either strategy alone produces.

Decision 5 — containment, and whether you actually need a VM

Your instinct was that a supervisor must live outside the crashing process, and therefore that fail-fast implies a VM. Erlang is the counter-example to your own worry: BEAM processes are not OS processes. Isolation comes from separate heaps, not separate address spaces — and that is a property Hey may already have.

Evidence found in the runtime today

hey_callable_bind deep-copies every captured value through hey_value_copy_isolated(), with the comment “the environment is thread-shared.” Hey already isolates values at actor and callable boundaries by copying. That is structurally the same guarantee BEAM buys with its VM — which means the supervisor may be affordable without one.

Decision 5

Where does the supervisor live?

Only matters if you pick D4-b. The others never crash.

The distinction that matters most on this page

A supervisor contains logic failures. It cannot contain memory corruption. A checked boundary rejecting "abc" is a controlled failure — safe to unwind and restart. An unsound uniqueness predicate mutating an array someone else still holds is not: it corrupts state that has already escaped, and no supervisor, VM or otherwise, can undo it. Decision 5 does not reduce the need to prove the predicate. The two are independent, and I want that on the record before either lands.

Cost of the one-implementation move, stated once

The mirror is currently our best bug detector — most of the silent wrong-answer classes killed this year surfaced as two-lane divergence. Deleting an arm removes that oracle. Survivable, but the replacement — spec goldens plus a negative test suite — has to exist before the arm goes, not after.

Decision 8 — two backends, and what the mirror actually buys

The argument that changed my mind

“We don't need native-c and llvm to tell us that 2.5 * 3 is not simply 6 — that's a bug cuz we know math.” This is right and it undercuts what I told you. The mirror is a RELATIVE oracle: it tells you the two lanes disagree, never which one is correct. It is blind to every bug where both lanes are wrong together. A spec that asserts 7.5 is an ABSOLUTE oracle — it catches the bug whether one lane is wrong or both, and it costs one implementation instead of two.

We have a live example: wfloatparam, where M.scale(2.5, 2) returns 4 compiled and 5.0 interpreted. The mirror flagged a disagreement. A spec asserting 5.0 would have named the defect.

Can native C do the unboxing and the in-place work? Yes — capability is not the blocker. heyc.c is an emitter; it writes LLVM text. Nothing stops C from emitting specialized IR that Hey also emits. The blocker is labour: every optimization written twice, in two languages, kept byte-identical — and byte-identity on a type-inference and escape analysis is far harder than byte-identity on straightforward lowering. The dead weight is real, but it is duplication, not impossibility.
