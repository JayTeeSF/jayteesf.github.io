The Five Things Hey Should Take From Mojo

Mojo is the fastest thing on earth with Python's syntax, and the obvious move is to copy what you can see: the ownership keywords, the compile-time tricks, the vector types. That is the wrong shopping list. The most valuable thing Mojo has is the part you cannot see from the syntax — the compiler substrate underneath it — plus one expensive lesson Mojo learned by shipping a feature and then taking it back.

Explored, not yet shipped

TL;DR

  • Steal Mojo's foundation, not its surface. Mojo is, in Modular's own words, “syntactic sugar for MLIR” — it lowers through a real, inspectable, verifiable multi-level intermediate representation. Hey already has three such levels, but they are print-only strings. Turning them into real IRs is the single highest-leverage borrow on this page.
  • The five, in order: (1) give the IR a parser so it round-trips; (2) lower ownership on the mid-level IR so immutable values update in place; (3) verify the IR instead of only diffing the output bytes; (4) lower by rewriting structure, not by scanning strings; (5) keep one surface language — put the strictness in the IR, not in a second syntax.
  • Disclosed weakness: none of this is shipped — it is a direction, not a changelog. And we are explicitly not adopting Mojo's actual compiler framework: MLIR is a C++ project, and bolting it under a Hey-written compiler would break Hey's self-hosting and its two-emitter safety net. The strongest argument for adopting it anyway — a mature backend for free — is one this note does not fully defeat.
  • Falsifiability (the cheapest first step is also the test): give Hey's mid-level IR a parser and assert print(parse(x)) == x across the existing golden corpus. If that ever fails on IR the byte-check calls clean, you have found a bug neither emitter could see.

First, what Mojo actually is

Two things “everyone knows” about Mojo are out of date. It is not a superset of Python — that was the 2023 pitch, and by 2026 the goal was abandoned or postponed indefinitely; Modular's current line is that Python compatibility is not the priority, “high-performance, portable compute” is. And it is not a peer of MLIR. It is built on MLIR: Mojo's compiler is “built using MLIR Core,” and the language “acts as syntactic sugar for MLIR.” Mojo turned 1.0 on 11 August 2026 and is now open source under Apache 2.0.

That is the reveal, and the whole reason this article exists. What Hey shares with Mojo is not syntax; it is shape — both reach LLVM through intermediate representations. The MLIR paper (Lattner, Amini, Bondhugula, Cohen, Davis, Pienaar, Riddle, Shpeisman, Vasilache, Zinenko; arXiv:2002.11054, 2020) even draws the problem it exists to solve, and Hey is in the drawing: Swift, Rust, and Julia each hand-building their own mid-level IR down to LLVM. Hey's Hey → HIR → MIR → LLVM is one more row of it. Chris Lattner built LLVM, then MLIR, then the company that put Mojo on top — the lineage is the relationship, and it is a substrate, not a syntax.

The five borrows

  1. Treat the IR as a product, not a byproduct — give it a parser. Mojo rests on an IR you can print, read, transform, and print again. Hey emits its mid-level IR as text but cannot read it back, and that missing direction is expensive: the compiler is checked today by diffing its bytes against a second, native emitter — which proves the two agree, and nothing about whether what they agree on is well-formed. A parser buys the invariant a second emitter cannot: print(parse(x)) == x, a producer that can't be wrong the same way the emitter is, because it never consults it.
  2. Make ownership explicit, then lower it on the mid-level IR. Mojo puts ownership in the surface language: arguments default to read-only, or mut, or var (owned), and ^ transfers a value, leaving the original uninitialised. Hey wants what those keywords enable, not the keywords. Hey is immutable and reference-counted — the exact setting of Perceus (Reinking, Xie, de Moura, Leijen — PLDI 2021), where a uniquely-owned value is updated in place. That is how an immutable language stops copying and reaches C speed — and, like a borrow checker, it belongs on the desugared mid-level IR, not the syntax tree.
  3. Verify the IR; do not only diff the output. MLIR builds correctness into the IR as op verifiers — invariants that run after every transformation and fail the instant a pass produces malformed IR. Hey's byte-for-byte check is blind in two places it has already been bitten by: it cannot catch two emitters wrong the same way, and it only sees inputs you run. A verifier over HIR and MIR — well-typedness, single-assignment dominance, reference-count balance — turns a whole- pipeline “the bytes differ” into a local “this operation breaks that invariant, here.” It covers what the two-emitter check structurally cannot.
  4. Lower by rewriting structure, not by scanning strings. Under Mojo, lowering is rewriting: match a shape, produce a lower one. Hey's LLVM emitter lowers with hand-written text scanners — the project's own census counts thirteen, eight named and five anonymous, each accepting a different slice of operators. That has a hard wall: splitting expressions by scanning left to right, it cannot express a right-associative operator like **. A small match-and-rewrite layer written in Hey, over the parsed IR from borrow #1, dissolves that wall — associativity becomes data the IR carries, not an accident of scan direction.
  5. Keep one surface language — learn this from Mojo's scar. Mojo once had two kinds of function: a strict, statically-typed fn and a dynamic def. By 2026 it collapsed them — every function now starts with def, and fn was deprecated. The lesson is not about keywords: splitting your language into a “fast dialect” and a “friendly dialect” is a tax users pay forever. Hey should keep one surface language and put the strictness where it belongs — in the IR and the verifier, not a second version of the syntax.

What you can do now: see that four of these five are one move — make each IR level a real, checkable object — and the fifth keeps that move out of the surface language.

The one we refuse, and the argument we can't fully beat

The tempting sixth item is “just adopt MLIR.” We are not going to, and the reason is specific. MLIR is a C++ and TableGen project; Hey is written in Hey and keeps a native second emitter purely as a safety net that the two must match. Putting a C++ compiler core under a Hey-written compiler breaks the self-hosting story and makes that safety net incoherent — there is no longer a second, independent emitter to disagree with. So we take the ideas, implemented in Hey, and leave the framework.

The steelman, stated honestly

The strongest case against us: MLIR would hand Hey a mature LLVM path, a pass manager, and diagnostics for free — replacing exactly the hand-written scanners we keep calling a bug source. And the MLIR paper says a bespoke per-language IR pipeline is the very redundancy it exists to kill. We do not defeat that on cost alone. We rest the refusal on one load-bearing constraint — self-hosting plus the two-emitter check — and note that if Hey ever relaxes either, this decision should be reopened.

Where to start

The order is not arbitrary. Borrow #1 — the parser and the round-trip invariant — is the cheapest and it unlocks the rest: you cannot safely mechanise rewrites (#4) or reuse analysis (#2) without an IR you can read, and you cannot trust a rewrite without a verifier (#3) to catch a malformed one. So the first commit is small and boring: teach Hey's mid-level IR to parse the text it already prints, and assert the two are inverses over the golden corpus. If that invariant ever trips on a program the byte-for-byte check calls clean, it will have found a class of bug that the entire two-emitter apparatus was built to find and, structurally, could not. That is the whole thesis in one test.
