Distributed Hey: what runs across machines today, and the cluster that is decided

Index Reference 3: Actors and failure Reference 4: Workers, Jobs, streams Reference 5: Distributed Reference 6: Standard library Reference 11: Roadmap

You have a laptop, a Linux box under the desk and a spare Mac mini. You'd like your Jobs to use all three without rewriting them. That is the goal the Maintainer ruled. This page is honest about the distance: the network pieces (nodes, encrypted links, remote actors, remote monitors) run today; the cluster that finds machines and places work on them for you is decided but not built.

On one screen Works today: two or more Hey processes connect over TCP or TLS, address each other's actors by remote pid, send and call with explicit outcomes, watch each other with remote monitors, and submit work to a named Job on a configured peer.1 The demo scripts run through the interpreter (heyc file.hey --run); on the 0.99.606a toolchain the ping-pong demo also compiles with hey build and runs natively, but compiling the whole distributed stack is still an open task. Not built: discovery, placement, clusterd, Jobs that spread themselves across machines, cluster supervision. There is no clustering yet.

1. The goal, as ruled: add a machine, existing Jobs use it

Decided, coming (ruled; not built). An operator admits a machine to a trusted pool through a simple setup. Existing eligible Jobs then use its capacity without application changes: no socket names, no hand-built peer records, no retry or checksum loops in your code. Synchronization, checksums, delivery checks, retries and recovery are Hey's job. Being on the network grants nothing by itself: discovery and authorized admission are separate steps. Hey will not promise exactly-once external effects; work that cannot be safely repeated must say so, and Hey must report uncertainty instead of inventing success. One-line summary in the decision ledger.

3. Run the demos

Two words first. A frame is one length-prefixed message on the wire. TLS is the standard encryption and certificate check that HTTPS uses. The repository ships six multi-process demos Works today: ping-pong (Remote.call), a remote actor (Remote.send into a named mailbox), a remote monitor (node B disappears, A's watcher hears NODE_DOWN), stale incarnation, Mandelbrot tiles farmed to worker processes, and a word count. Each starts node B in the background and node A in the foreground and prefixes their lines with [B] and [A]. These commands were run from a copy of examples/distributed with HEYC pointing at the toolchain's heyc and HEY_STDLIB_PATH at its stdlib.

4. Honest delivery rules

Networks lose things. Hey's distributed modules never hide that behind a retry you did not ask for.

Predict it. Node A calls Remote.call on an actor on node B with a 2-second timeout, and gets a timeout. Did B do the work? Answer: unknown. The request may have been lost on the way, B may have done it and the reply was lost, or B may still be doing it. That is why Hey never retries a call for you: a retry could do the work twice.
RuleWhat it means for your codeStatus
Send is at most once. Success means "accepted for transport": framed and written to the connection.It does not mean the remote actor processed it. No silent retries, no deduplication inside Remote.send.Works today
A call is a send plus a matching reply, bounded by a timeout.A timeout can follow successful remote processing. Treat it as unknown, exactly like Actor.call locally.Works today
A missing Job reply is outcome_unknown.Never resubmit blindly; carry your own id.Works today
Peer loss is suspicion.A node cannot tell a network partition (the network between two healthy machines is cut) from a dead peer. A remote monitor's down notice does not cancel or retry your work by itself.Works today
No global ordering between different senders, connections or reconnects.Bytes on one connection stay in order; handler completion does not. Need business order? Use a partition key and one sequenced reducer.Decided, coming (as the written contract)

5. Who owns what

6. Durability through hey_durable_log

Works today in the package hey_durable_log (not in the standard library). It appends records locally, replicates them to a quorum (a majority of the replicas), returns quorum_durable only after that majority confirms, fences a stale leader (a numbered term lets replicas refuse writes from a leader that has been replaced), and repairs divergent tails (the last few records a replica has that the majority never accepted). A missing majority returns an error; it never quietly downgrades to "saved locally". One catch: a quorum error may still leave a local append, so it is not proof that no record exists. What a durable log costs in RAM and time is in Acknowledging RAM: what a durable log costs.

Wiring it under Jobs is part of task 32: Hey consumes the provider's facts (who has authority, what is committed) and does not reimplement election or quorum arithmetic.

8. Sync for devices

For phones and TVs that go offline, stdlib:Sync merges records field by field with clocks and revisions (a CRDT-style merge: two edits to different fields both survive). It is a standard-library module, documented in Reference 6: The standard library.
