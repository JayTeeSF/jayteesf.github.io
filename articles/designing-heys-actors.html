Designing Hey's Actors

Hey wants millions of actors running on a handful of threads. Rather than guess its way there, the team turned its own research into a literal roadmap — a document titled "What Makes an Actor Cheap?" that named the exact papers and runtimes to crib from. This is the story of what got decided, where each idea was stolen from, and what it costs.

TL;DR

  • Per-actor memory today: ~403 bytes, measured. A diet slice in flight targets ~303 bytes. Both are still short of Pony's 240 bytes and Tokio's 64 bytes — well ahead of Go's 2,048-byte goroutine and BEAM's ~2,616-byte process, but the honest gap to the leaders remains open.
  • The founding finding: fast is "no stack," not "immutable." Tokio (mutable, 64 B) and Pony (mutable, 240 B) get cheap by refusing to let a handler pause mid-call. BEAM (immutable, ~2,616 B) pays a real stack anyway, for the identical reason Go (mutable, 2,048 B) does: both let a worker suspend anywhere.
  • The receive-placement question flipped twice in one working day. Research recommended Pony's top-only receive; the team briefly planned mid-body multi-receive with its own compiler pass; the final ruling landed back on top-only — and the abandoned research was kept on file rather than thrown away.
  • Immutability deletes machinery neither Pony nor BEAM got to skip. No capability type system, no ORCA garbage collector, no per-message deep copy — sharing a value across actors becomes bumping a reference count, safe by construction because nothing can ever mutate it.
  • A 100,000-actor Skynet-style spawn tree already runs correctly today in roughly 15–21 seconds, and the measured bottleneck is a quadratic list-append, not actor-spawn cost — worth knowing before crediting or blaming the actor design for that number.
  • Disclosed risk: the reference-counting plan's own source paper measured what happens without its compiler-static optimizations — naive, atomic-everywhere counting costs 5–59% slower, depending on workload. The plan only pays off if the single-owner fast path stays fast.

How to read this article

Every number below traces to a sourced research brief written during Hey's stack-removal campaign, or to a primary source those briefs cite — official docs, runtime source code, or a peer-reviewed paper — linked inline where it's used, plus a full source list at the end. Technical terms get a definition with an example the first time they show up, boxed like this one. There's a glossary at the bottom if you lose track.

1. The goal: an actor system built from a reading list

Term: what's an actor?

An actor is a small, independent worker that owns its own private state and talks to other actors only by sending messages — never by reaching into another actor's data directly. Picture a hotel front desk clerk: guests slide requests across the counter, the clerk handles them one at a time, and nobody walks behind the counter to grab the ledger. A well-designed actor system can run millions of these clerks, each cheap enough to spawn that doing so costs about as much as allocating a small struct.

Hey, a young, immutable-first language, wants that: work-stealing over one OS thread per core, actors spawnable by the million, backed by a structural edge no other actor runtime gets for free — reference-shared immutable messages needing zero copying and zero locking to share safely. That's the goal statement from the team's own synthesis of its research.

What makes this project unusual isn't the goal — every actor runtime chases some version of it — it's the discipline behind how Hey is getting there. Before writing a line of the new runtime, the team wrote "What Makes an Actor Cheap?", a sourced comparison of BEAM, Pony, Go, and Tokio's per-actor cost. That article wasn't published as background reading. It became the literal work-order: a "stack-removal campaign" document divides Hey's actor rework into workstreams, and every one of them names a specific paper or piece of runtime source as its "crib" — the thing to copy from, not reinvent. This article walks through what that reading list produced: the decisions, where each one came from, and what each one costs.

2. The reveal: the stack is the cost, not the data model

Term: call stack

A call stack is the pile of "what do I do when I'm done with this" bookmarks a program keeps as functions call other functions — sticky notes added every time you get interrupted by a sub-task, popped off when that sub-task finishes. A worker that can pause mid-function to wait on a message needs a real, growable stack to remember where it was. A worker that only ever runs one message handler to completion before yielding needs no stack at all, because there's nothing mid-function left to remember.

The founding research laid four runtimes side by side and found a pattern that has nothing to do with whether the language is mutable or immutable. Tokio's tasks cost 64 bytes and have no stack — "under the hood, they require only a single allocation," designed explicitly so that "spawning thousands, or even millions of tasks" is normal. — Tokio Tutorial: Spawning. Pony's actors cost ~240 bytes and also have no stack: "actors do not have individual stacks, rather they use the stack of the OS thread they are scheduled on." — Pony Runtime FAQ. Both are mutable languages. Go's goroutines, also mutable, cost 2,048 bytes initially because they carry a real, growable stack that can suspend at any depth — runtime/stack.go. And BEAM's Erlang processes, fully immutable, cost ~2,616 bytes (327 words) for the identical reason Go pays: a process can block on a receive in the middle of any function, so it needs a stack too — Erlang System Documentation.

Restated as the research brief puts it: "fast is 'no stack,' not 'immutable.'" That one sentence is the hinge the rest of this article turns on — it is why Hey's actor redesign spends its effort removing a stack, not chasing a particular data model, and it's why the very first decision below has nothing to do with immutability at all.

3. Decision one: no stack, run actors to completion

Hey's actors are being moved off individual OS threads entirely. Today, spawning an actor means pthread_create — one OS thread and one C stack per actor, a design the runtime's own source comment admits is temporary. The replacement already exists in skeleton form: a pooled path that runs actors as short step functions over a fixed worker pool, the same shape Tokio and Pony use.

Pro

It's the only design with a shipping proof at the target floor. Pony reaches ~240 bytes and Tokio 64 bytes by this exact mechanism; nothing that keeps a per-actor stack has ever hit those numbers. For a goal measured in millions of actors, "proven, not promising" matters more than elegance.

Con

A handler can't pause mid-work to wait for another message — there's no stack to suspend into. Code that used to block partway through has to be restructured as self-messaging: finish what you can, send yourself (or another actor) a message, and resume from the top next time. And any call that genuinely blocks — file I/O, DNS — can't just sit there occupying a scheduler thread; it needs a separate pool to run on, the same shape as BEAM's dirty scheduler, a fixed-size pool (BEAM defaults dirty I/O schedulers to 10, independent of core count) reserved for exactly this kind of work so it never stalls the actors that don't need it. — erl_nif docs

4. Decision two: receive can only appear at the top of the loop

Term: receive & selective receive

Receive is the operation an actor uses to pull its next message off its own queue. Selective receive (Erlang's term) lets an actor skip past messages it doesn't want yet, leaving them in the mailbox for later — like flipping past junk mail to find the one envelope you're waiting on, then going back for the rest afterward. It's expressive, but it means the actor has to be able to pause in the exact middle of a function while it waits for that one right message to show up.

This is the decision everything else in Hey's actor redesign sequences behind, and it's also the one that got argued out loud rather than settled quietly. A dedicated research brief laid out a decision table with three options: loop-top-only receive (Pony's model), mid-body suspension via LLVM coroutines, or an emitter-level compiler transform in the style of Rust, Kotlin, or C#. The table's own verdict was blunt: loop-top-only is "the only option with a shipping proof at the target floor," and the cheapest to build — no new compiler IR, no state-machine pass.

The counter-example is Erlang, and it's a clean one. Selective receive is exactly what forces every BEAM process to carry a growable stack in the first place — and it isn't free at runtime either: Erlang's own mailbox implements selective receive with a "save queue" that non-matching messages sit in, which means a receive can cost an O(N) scan of everything currently queued if the message it wants isn't near the front. — Erlang System Documentation

Here's where the story gets interesting rather than settled: the team didn't just read the recommendation and take it. The maintainer initially ruled the other way — mid-body, multi-receive semantics were declared "planned," with a whole compiler workstream sketched out to support it: a state-machine transform that would turn each receive into a state boundary, hoist only the locals that stay alive across it, and dispatch resumption by state, borrowing liveness-analysis ideas from rustc's own coroutine pass. That plan was scoped seriously, with its own acceptance gate: a pre-registered ceiling on how many bytes a suspended actor's frame could grow to, aimed squarely at Rust's own cautionary tale — an early async state machine that ballooned to 400 kilobytes in one real codebase, and at least one embedded team that reverted async code back to plain blocking calls specifically to cut binary size in half. — Mandry, "How Rust optimizes async/await", Rust Project Goals 2026

Then, later the same day, that plan was struck. The final ruling: top-only receive is the design. The multi-receive workstream's gates were withdrawn, and the state-machine research itself wasn't deleted — it was explicitly kept on file as background material, in case the tradeoff gets revisited later. It's a good example of a team taking a considered swing at more expressive semantics, pricing out what it would actually cost, and choosing to bank the research rather than either force the feature in or throw the work away.

Pro

Actors stay tiny — no suspended-frame bookkeeping to size or grow. And causal message ordering falls out for free: because a receive always starts fresh at the top with nothing pinned mid-flight, there's no "half-finished, waiting on a specific reply" state to reason about.

Con

It's less expressive than Erlang's model. You can't pause mid-flow waiting for one specific reply among several possible messages; a flow that needs that shape has to be re-expressed as explicit state carried between separate top-level receives. That's a real restructuring cost for some programs, not just a syntax change.

5. Decision three: mailboxes as an intrusive Vyukov queue

Term: MPSC queue

MPSC stands for multi-producer, single-consumer: many senders can push into the queue at once, but only one reader ever pops from it — which is exactly an actor's mailbox, since many other actors may message it but only the actor itself ever reads its own queue. An intrusive queue goes one step further: instead of allocating a separate queue-node wrapper for every message, the message struct itself doubles as the node, so enqueuing costs zero extra allocation.

Hey's mailbox is moving from a mutex-guarded linked list to an intrusive, lock-free MPSC queue based on the design published by Dmitry Vyukov: push is a single atomic exchange plus a plain store, with no retry loop; pop needs no atomics at all on the fast path. — 1024cores, Vyukov's own site, int08h, "Ode to a Vyukov Queue". It's a proven design elsewhere: Rust's futures-rs channel credits Vyukov directly in its source, and cordyceps::MpscQueue is a published intrusive implementation of the same idea. Hey gets an unusually easy migration here: its existing message struct is already shaped as {value, next} — already an intrusive queue node, just not wired up lock-free yet.

Pro

Sends become wait-free — no lock, no retry, one atomic operation. And because the message struct already has the right shape, there's zero additional per-message allocation: the node the queue needs and the message the actor wanted to send are the same object.

Con

The algorithm has a known, accepted soft spot: a producer that's been preempted between its atomic exchange and its follow-up store leaves a momentary "hole" that the consumer has to spin past — "extremely small" in Vyukov's own description, but real, and the consumer's spin is part of the design, not a bug to fix. Separately, an unbounded mailbox is a real hazard on its own: Pony shipped without any backpressure at all, and had to retrofit a mechanism called "muting" — descheduling senders to an overloaded actor — only after real production failures surfaced the gap. — ponylang/ponyc PR #2264. Hey has to decide bounded-vs-muting at design time, not retrofit it the way Pony did — a mailbox with no limit at all is a bit like a group chat nobody ever leaves: fine for a while, and then very much not.

6. Decision four: a work-stealing scheduler, cribbed from Tokio and Go

Term: work-stealing scheduler

A work-stealing scheduler runs one worker thread per CPU core, each with its own queue of runnable work. When a worker's queue runs dry, it steals work from a busier worker's queue instead of sitting idle. Picture four cashiers with separate lines: instead of standing around empty-handed, an idle cashier walks over and takes the next customer from the longest line.

A direct read of Hey's own runtime source shows it's already shaped like this in skeleton: a per-worker-queue-plus-shared-injection-queue design, with spinning/parked/steal counters already being tracked. What it doesn't have yet is the part that makes the shape actually pay off. The measured smoking gun: today, every message sent from outside a worker thread — effectively all of them under load — is forced through one single global mutex. One orchestrator trace showed 20,000 out of 20,000 sends routed through that lock, against just 448 handled locally and 2 successful steals, with a park/unpark cycle paid on nearly every message.

The fix is to crib the mechanics that Tokio and Go already proved out: a fixed 256-slot lock-free ring per worker with a packed atomic head (so the owner popping and a thief stealing never block each other), a steal-half policy that claims roughly half a victim's queue in one atomic swoop to amortize the synchronization cost, a one-task LIFO slot for cache-hot handoff between a sender and the actor it just woke, and a searching throttle that caps at most half the workers hunting for work at once so an idle system doesn't wake everyone at once. — Tokio queue.rs, Tokio's 2019 scheduler post. Go's per-P runnext slot is the same idea under a different name, with its own anti-starvation rule: thieves get four tries at a victim's queue, and only the fourth may take its runnext. — Go runtime/proc.go

Pro

Cores stay busy without funneling every send through one shared bottleneck — directly fixing the exact contention the 20,000-to-448 trace exposed, and the counters to prove it (mailbox_lock_wait_ns, steal_attempts) already exist in Hey's runtime today, waiting to be driven to zero.

Con

Work-stealing schedulers have a well-documented trap shelf, which is exactly why cribbing proven implementations rather than inventing one matters: thundering-herd wakeups if too many idle workers wake at once for the same bit of new work, and a real, documented lost-wakeup race in Tokio's own history — its unpark path uses an unconditional swap rather than a compare-and-swap specifically because a CAS racing a spurious wake can silently drop a genuine wakeup notification. — Tokio park.rs. Getting either wrong doesn't crash anything; it just quietly stalls a worker or wakes a stampede, which is the kind of bug that's brutal to notice and worse to reproduce.

7. Decision five: zero-copy immutable messages, paid for with a reference count

Term: reference counting

Reference counting means every shared value carries a small counter of how many places currently hold a pointer to it; sharing the value bumps the counter instead of copying the data, and the value is freed only once the counter hits zero. It's the difference between mailing someone a photocopy of a document versus mailing them a library card that lets them read the same original — the card is far cheaper to produce, but now the library has to track how many cards are out there.

This is the decision where immutability actually does the work, rather than being an innocent bystander. Two papers anchor it, for two different reasons.

The reference-counting mechanism itself is modeled on Perceus (Reinking, Xie, de Moura, Leijen — PLDI 2021), Koka's precise-refcounting scheme. Perceus tracks ownership of every value linearly through the whole program and has the compiler statically insert increment/decrement operations at each use, rather than leaning on a runtime garbage collector. Its headline result: on a 42-million-insertion red-black tree benchmark, purely functional Koka code ran within 10% of hand-mutated C++'s std::map, while a comparable Java version used roughly 10x the memory (1.7 GiB vs. 170 MiB) for similar speed. — Perceus, PLDI 2021 (PDF)

The other paper explains why Hey gets to use a simpler version of this idea than Pony did. Pony also shares data across actors by reference with zero copying — but Pony's data is mutable, so it needed an entire compile-time capability type system plus a purpose-built garbage collector, ORCA (Clebsch et al. — OOPSLA 2017), just to make that safe. In the paper's own words: "In a purely functional actor language such as Erlang... it would be trivial to implement a collector such as ours... One of the early decisions in the design of Pony was to support mutable data structures and, for efficiency reasons, to implement zero-copy message passing. Mutability introduce[s] challenges..." — ORCA, §1–2 (PDF). Under immutable data, nothing can ever race on a write, so Hey skips the capability system and the collector entirely and still gets the zero-copy sharing — sending a large value becomes bumping a counter rather than copying anything.

This isn't a hypothetical saving. Hey's own measurements of its current, un-optimized value handling at a modest scale (46-key objects, mostly small integers and short strings) show roughly 124 cloning operations and 204 releases per request under today's copy-everything approach — overhead that shows up as a measured throughput gap of about ~47.8 microseconds per request against Go on the same workload. — tagged-value representation brief, bench repo, 2026-07-26.

That request-level number is the everyday case. At the scale that actually motivates paying for a reference-counting rewrite, the same copy-everything approach gets far worse, not just proportionally worse: a dedicated single-threaded probe measured reading one 4,096-element value out of an object at ~8,198 hidden clone operations per access (about 2 per element — the deep-clone signature) and ~48.5 microseconds per access, against just 215 nanoseconds and 7.0 clones for a plain integer access measured on the same probe. Both numbers are real, measured at different scales, and both matter: the 46-key, mostly-small-values case above is what a typical request pays today; the 4,096-element case is the reference-large motivation for why this decision exists at all — the bigger and more nested the shared value, the worse copy-everything gets, and collapsing "copy" into "bump a counter" is designed to make that curve flat instead of linear. — bench-repo measurement, PER-ACCESS DECOMPOSITION PROBE, 2026-07-26 ~14:20 PDT, bench/probes/access-decomposition.hey (counter-attributed, checksummed; no public URL — internal bench log).

That's the shape of cost the refcounting plan targets directly at both scales: most of those clones exist only because nothing currently distinguishes "share a reference" from "copy the value," and immutability is what makes collapsing that distinction safe.

Pro

Sharing a large immutable value across actors goes from "copy the whole thing" to "increment a counter" — the exact class of overhead measured above (124 clones and ~47.8 µs at 46 keys; 8,198 clones and ~48.5 µs reading a single 4,096-element value) becomes close to free, with no type system and no cycle collector required, because immutable data has no mutation to guard against and no cycles a naive counter can't already handle.

Con

Every value now carries a counter field — a real, permanent size and bookkeeping cost on every heap object, not a one-time price. And the fast path has to actually stay fast: Perceus's own authors measured what happens if you skip its compiler-static optimizations (drop specialization, reuse analysis) and count references the naive, always-atomic way — 5% to 59% slower, depending on workload, with disabling reuse analysis and drop specialization alone costing more than 2x on their red-black tree benchmark. The win is real, but only if the single-owner fast path is built with the same care the paper put into it, not bolted on as an afterthought. — Perceus §4

8. The payoff: what immutability buys, and where the campaign stands

Lay the five decisions next to each other and a shape appears that neither BEAM nor Pony gets in one package. BEAM has immutability but still pays the stack tax (receive-anywhere) and, mostly-but-not- entirely for free, a deep-copy tax on every message. Pony has no-stack, cheap actors, but pays an entire capability-type-system-plus-garbage-collector tax to share mutable data safely. Hey's bet is that a language built immutable-first from day one can take the cheap half of each — no-stack, run-to-completion actors; a lock-free mailbox; a work-stealing scheduler; and reference-counted, zero-copy sharing — without paying either runtime's extra tax, because immutability was the thing that made both of those taxes necessary in the first place.

None of this is finished, and the team isn't pretending otherwise. As of today: the actor record measures ~403 bytes, with a struct-diet slice in flight targeting ~303 bytes — real progress, but still short of Pony's 240 and further still from Tokio's 64. The mailbox migration to the lock-free Vyukov queue and the scheduler's move off its single global injection lock are underway, sequenced so the mailbox work lands first and the scheduler work builds on top of it. A blocking-work bridge pool, sized on BEAM's dirty-scheduler model, is scoped as independent, lower-risk work that can land in parallel with everything else. And the abandoned mid-body-receive research mentioned above is deliberately still on file, not deleted, in case the tradeoff is ever worth revisiting.

For a sense of where things stand today, not where they'll land: a 100,000-actor spawn-and-sum tree, in the style of the well-known Skynet benchmark, already runs and produces correct sums on Hey's current build, completing in roughly 15 to 21 seconds across repeated runs. The interesting finding isn't the wall-clock number itself — it's that the measured bottleneck is a quadratic cost in how results get appended back up the tree, not the cost of spawning the actors. That matters for honesty about the campaign above: none of the five decisions in this article are what's currently limiting that number, which means the eventual byte-count and scheduler wins described here haven't been measured against it yet either. The reading list produced a design. It hasn't yet produced a finished runtime.

Glossary

actor
An independent unit of computation with private state that communicates only by passing messages, never by sharing memory.
call stack
The pile of "return to here next" bookmarks kept as functions call other functions; only needed by workers that can suspend mid-function.
run-to-completion
A scheduling model where a handler always runs to its end (or to the top of its next loop) before yielding, so it never needs a stack to suspend into.
receive
The operation an actor uses to pull its next message off its own mailbox.
selective receive
Skipping past unwanted messages in a mailbox and leaving them for later; requires a per-actor stack to pause mid-function.
mailbox
The queue of messages an actor has received but not yet processed.
MPSC queue
A queue with many producers (senders) and a single consumer (the actor reading its own mailbox).
intrusive queue
A queue where the message itself doubles as the queue's linked-list node, avoiding a separate allocation per message.
backpressure
A mechanism that slows or blocks senders to an overloaded receiver, preventing an unbounded queue from growing without limit.
work-stealing scheduler
A scheduler where an idle worker thread takes queued work from a busier thread's queue instead of sitting idle.
reference counting
Tracking how many places hold a pointer to a shared value, freeing it only once that count reaches zero, so sharing costs a counter update instead of a copy.
reference capability
Pony's compile-time tag on a reference, restricting how it may be read or written, used to guarantee at most one actor can mutate a given object at a time.
immutability
A value that, once created, can never be changed — only replaced — which removes the need to guard against concurrent mutation when the value is shared.
dirty scheduler
BEAM's term for a separate, fixed-size thread pool reserved for blocking or long-running native calls, kept off the main reduction-counted schedulers.
