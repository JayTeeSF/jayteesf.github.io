Hey · decision docket · 1 August 2026

Arithmetic Hey never decided — and one piece of housekeeping

Eight decisions. Seven come from measuring the most boring arithmetic we could write, in both lanes, against Ruby. Every one of them is a choice the language already made by accident — nobody chose it, it fell out of the implementation. The eighth is a disk-and-time question that is now costing real minutes.

How to use this page

Pick an option in each box. At the bottom there is a Copy ruling button that produces a short text block — paste it back to the orchestrator and that is the whole ruling. You do not have to answer all eight; anything left blank stays open and nothing is blocked on it today.

Nothing here is a bug report. Bugs are being fixed without asking you. These are places where two reasonable languages disagree and Hey has to pick one.

What was measured, and how

A conformance battery ran 93 arithmetic behaviours through both of Hey's execution paths — the interpreter (hey script.hey) and a compiled binary (heyc build, which is what users actually ship) — and compared each against Ruby 4.0.6 on the same machine. The Linux seat independently reproduced the key rows on different hardware.

The arithmetic decisions

Decision 9

Which way does integer division round?

Hey currently answers -7 / 2 as -3. Ruby answers -4. This differs only when the signs disagree.

Measured, both platforms

-7 / 2 → -3  ·  (-7) % 3 → -1  ·  7 % (-3) → 1. Ruby gives -4, 2, -2. Hey's two execution paths agree with each other on every one of these — so this is a language choice, not a bug, and it was never actually chosen.

Decision 10

Does % follow the divisor's sign?

Follows from Decision 9, but is separately decidable — and it decides whether Hey gets one modulo operator or two.

Decision 11

says 100.0 prints 1e2

The most user-visible item in the whole battery. Both Hey paths agree; Ruby prints 100.0.

Scope

10.0→1e1, 100.0→1e2, 1000.0→1e3, 1500.0→1.5e3. Not a bug and not a lane divergence — both paths agree, on both operating systems. Purely a rendering decision.

Decision 12

What happens when an integer gets too big?

Hey silently wraps around: the largest integer plus one becomes the most negative integer.

Newly proven, and it changes this box

The wrapping is defined behaviour, not undefined, and both Hey paths produce identical results. The compiler emits plain add instructions without the flag that would make overflow undefined. So this is a genuine open choice, not a latent crash — which is why it is on this page rather than in the fix queue.

Decision 13

Three small gaps, one question

7.5 % 2 refuses. 3.to_f() does not exist. Both fail honestly rather than answering wrongly — the question is whether to fill them.

One wrinkle worth knowing

7.5 % 2 refuses when written literally — but the same expression through a function parameter currently answers 1, because floats get quietly narrowed to integers at that boundary. That part is a bug and is being fixed regardless of this decision. It is mentioned because it is why "the gap is closed" would have been the wrong reading of a literals-only test.

Decision 14

Dividing a float by zero, and converting infinity

Two places where Hey refuses and Ruby answers. Both are about whether "no sensible answer" should stop the program.

Housekeeping

Decision 15

The worktrees — this one now costs measurable time

40 scratch checkouts, 4.6 GB, 77 cached compiler binaries. Oldest untouched since 24 July.

Why this stopped being housekeeping

Each scratch checkout keeps its own copy of the compiler and, until today's fix, each one paid a ~3.5 minute rebuild of the compiler from scratch. The Linux machine has 12 and paid it three times in one morning without realising what it was. Stale checkouts have also been the direct cause of three build hangs — they get walked by tools that scan the source tree, one of which enumerated 284,760 files to find 2,684 real ones.

Not on this page

Being fixed without asking you

The 44 places where Hey's two execution paths disagree are defects, not choices, and are in the queue. The worst of them: arithmetic on a value read from a map concatenates instead of adding in compiled binaries — totals['count'] + 2 gives 72 where it should give 9. App teams have been warned with a workaround.

A further set of decisions — whether maps guarantee insertion order, what nil's relationship to numbers is, how strict number-from-text conversion should be — are real but have not been measured yet. They will get their own page rather than being guessed at here.
